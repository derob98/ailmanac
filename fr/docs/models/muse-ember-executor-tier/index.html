Muse Ember: The Executor Tier and the Band Nobody Priced

Intermédiaire

On 9 November 2026 Meta Superintelligence Labs released Muse Ember (muse-ember-1.0): its smallest model, pitched squarely at "the work agents repeat all day — tool calls, coding subtasks, routing, extraction, and long document parsing." It arrived at $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output.

That is not a coincidental number. It is, to the cent, Claude Haiku 5.5's rate card. Meta's launch table compares Muse Ember against Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna, and nothing else.

So the interesting question is not "is it cheap." Both are. The interesting question is the one neither launch page answers: given identical headline prices, which one actually bills you less? The answer turns out to depend entirely on how long your prompts get — because both models are priced by prompt length, and their thresholds are in different places.

What you'll learn
  • Read the three-band price table that Haiku 5.5 and Muse Ember jointly create — and find the 100K-200K window where Ember costs exactly one fifth of Haiku on input and output, and one twelfth on cache reads
  • Budget an agent loop by PEAK context rather than average context, because a threshold crossing reprices the request that crossed it
  • Interpret Meta's own benchmark table honestly: eight rows won, one row lost by 7.7 points, and a tie on the headline index
  • Spot the harness asymmetry buried in Meta's footnote 1 — the Terminal-Bench row compares three different agent harnesses, not three models
  • Know what Meta did not publish: no parameter count, no model card, no reasoning-effort ladder, no open weights
  • Decide whether Ember belongs in your stack as an executor under a planner, which is what both Meta and the independent evidence actually support
Dernière vérification : 2026-11-10.

Prices and benchmark scores are one day old and vendor-published. The >200K band ($0.30 / $0.012 / $1.50) appears in footnote 2 of Meta's post, not in its body — confirm it on Meta's current model page before you budget against it. Whether that band reprices the whole request or only the tokens above the line is not stated by Meta; this page flags it as an open question rather than resolving it. Benchmark rows marked "Meta internal" have no third-party reproduction. Audio and video input are Meta's claim; independent listings at the time of writing record text and image input with text output only. Parameter count, architecture, knowledge cutoff, cache-write pricing, batch pricing and rate limits are undisclosed.

Sources : Meta's launch post (research.meta.ai/blog/muse-ember), including its footnotes 1 and 2; Haiku 5.5 prices from Anthropic's rate card as documented in AILmanac's own Haiku 5.5 page; independent benchmark and verbosity figures from Artificial Analysis as reported by OrcaRouter's Muse Ember analysis.

The one-paragraph version​

Muse Ember is a closed-weight, reasoning-capable, long-context executor model with a 1M-token context window and a 512K output ceiling, priced at $0.10 / $0.004 cached / $0.50 per million tokens for prompts up to 200,000 tokens, stepping 3x on all three rates above that. Meta positions it as the bottom rung of a three-model ladder — Muse Wonder plans, Muse Spark codes, Muse Ember does the volume — and its own evaluations put it in a tie with Claude Haiku 5.5 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (36 vs 36) at roughly a third of the cost per task ($0.136 vs $0.411). It wins eight of Meta's ten benchmark rows and loses one that matters to anyone shipping code.

Part 1 — The three-band table​

Here is the thing the launch coverage missed. Haiku 5.5 and Muse Ember have the same headline price and the same 1M context window, and both switch to an expensive rate card once a prompt gets long. But Haiku's threshold is at 100,000 tokens and multiplies by 5x; Ember's is at 200,000 tokens and multiplies by 3x.

Line them up and the headline price stops being the story:

Prompt lengthHaiku 5.5 in / out / cache readMuse Ember in / out / cache readEmber's advantage
≤ 100K tokens$0.10 / $0.50 / $0.01$0.10 / $0.50 / $0.004Tie on in/out; 2.5x cheaper cache reads
100K – 200K tokens$0.50 / $2.50 / $0.05$0.10 / $0.50 / $0.0045x cheaper in/out; 12.5x cheaper cache reads
> 200K tokens$0.50 / $2.50 / $0.05$0.30 / $1.50 / $0.0121.67x cheaper in/out; 4.17x cheaper cache reads

All figures per million tokens. Haiku's bands are from Anthropic's rate card; Ember's from footnote 2 of Meta's launch post.

Three things fall out of that table, and none of them are visible from either vendor's announcement.

The middle band is where the decision lives​

Between 100,000 and 200,000 tokens of prompt, Haiku 5.5 has already repriced and Muse Ember has not. In that window the two models that advertise an identical price differ by exactly 5x on input and output and 12.5x on cache reads.

This is not an exotic window. A coding agent that has read a few files, a test log and a build output is sitting in it by the middle of a session. If you are routing high-volume subagent work and your transcripts habitually land between 100K and 200K tokens, this single band is the largest cost lever available to you — larger than prompt trimming, larger than caching strategy, larger than effort tuning.

Above 200K, the gap narrows far more than you would guess​

Once both models are on their expensive cards, Ember's advantage collapses from 5x to 1.67x on input and output. If your workload lives entirely above 200K tokens, the two are much closer than the band-2 arithmetic suggests, and other factors — the FrontierCode result below, output verbosity, latency, which cloud you are already on — should decide it instead of price.

Cache reads are the quiet win​

Ember's cached-input rate of $0.004/M is the aggressive number in the whole comparison — Meta frames it as a 96% discount off its own input rate. Against Haiku's $0.01 below the cliff that is 2.5x; against Haiku's $0.05 above it, 12.5x.

This matters disproportionately for agent loops, because an agent re-sends a stable prefix (system prompt, tool schemas) on every single step. That prefix caches. What does not cache is the growing transcript, which bills as fresh input every step. So a long agent run is mostly cheap cache reads plus an expensive, growing tail — and the cache-read rate multiplies across every step of every run.

Watch out
  • Meta's footnote says only that 'for longer prompts, Muse Ember costs $0.30' — it does not say whether the higher rate applies to the whole request or just the tokens above 200,000. Haiku 5.5's documented behaviour is whole-request repricing. Until Meta states otherwise, budget for whole-request repricing on Ember too: it is the conservative assumption, and it is the one consistent with the precedent next door.

Part 2 — Budget by peak context, not average context​

A threshold that reprices a request has a consequence that averages hide completely: the only number that matters is the high-water mark of a single request.

Consider a 20-step agent loop. Each step re-sends the system prompt, the tool schemas and the accumulated transcript. The prefix is stable and caches at $0.004/M. The transcript grows every step and bills fresh.

Guided walkthrough1 of 5
  1. Log the input token count of every request in a run and keep the maximum. A run averaging 90K tokens can easily peak at 210K on its final step — and on a prompt-length rate card, that final step may be billed at the expensive rate rather than the average one.
  2. Instrument the loop so you know the step index where input tokens pass 200,000 (Ember) or 100,000 (Haiku). That step, and every step after it, is the expensive part of the bill. Everything before it is nearly free by comparison.
  3. A single build log or test dump is often what pushes a run across a threshold. A fixed cap on each tool result shrinks the transcript without rewriting the stable prefix that your cache depends on — so it saves band money and cache money at once.
  4. Once your peak reliably exceeds the threshold, a model with a lower headline price and a worse cliff can lose to one with a higher headline price and no cliff at all. Re-run the comparison at your actual peak, not at the rate-card price.
  5. Cost per token is not cost per task. A model that needs 28 steps where another needs 20 can be more expensive at half the per-token price. This is the measurement that decides executor choice, and neither vendor publishes it.

Log peak input tokens per run so you know which band you are actually billed in

# Pseudocode for any agent loop, any provider.
# The number you need is the MAX, not the mean.

peak_input = 0
for step in run:
  resp = call_model(messages=transcript, tools=schemas)
  peak_input = max(peak_input, resp.usage.input_tokens)
  transcript.append(resp)

print("peak input tokens:", peak_input)
# Haiku 5.5  -> expensive card above 100_000
# Muse Ember -> expensive card above 200_000

Part 3 — Reading Meta's benchmark table honestly​

Meta published ten comparison rows, all at maximum reasoning effort, against Claude Haiku 5.5 and GPT-6 Luna. Ember wins eight, ties one, and loses one.

Four observations that change how you read it.

The AutomationBench gap is the real headline, and it is a different claim than "it codes well"​

37.8% against Haiku's 13.9% is a 2.7x ratio and by far the widest margin on the board. AutomationBench-AA scores business-workflow completion under strict pass@1. That result supports a specific claim — Ember is unusually good at finishing multi-step procedural work — and it is the strongest evidence for the executor framing. It says nothing about code quality.

FrontierCode is the row to take seriously​

Ember 27.2%, Haiku 34.9% — a 7.7-point loss, and the only row Ember loses. FrontierCode 2.0-AA scores real engineering tasks, and Meta included the row rather than omitting it, which is to its credit.

Put it next to Coding Agent Index-AA, where Ember wins 46 to 37, and a coherent picture emerges: Ember is better at driving a coding agent loop and worse at producing the code that has to survive review. That is exactly the profile of an executor and exactly the wrong profile for the model that writes your production diff.

The Terminal-Bench near-tie compares three harnesses, not three models​

Meta's footnote 1 is the most important sentence on the page. The Terminal-Bench 4.0 row ran each model in its own best published harness:

  • Muse Ember → mini-swe-agent
  • Claude Haiku 5.5 → Claude Code
  • GPT-6 Luna → Codex

A 40.9-vs-39.2 result across three different agent scaffolds is not a 1.7-point model difference. Harness choice moves agentic scores by several points on its own, which is precisely the trap How to read an AI benchmark without getting fooled is about. Treat this row as "roughly comparable," not as a win.

The index is a tie; the efficiency is the win​

Meta's own table marks both Ember and Haiku 5.5 as the best result at 36 on the Intelligence Index. The underlying scatter is slightly less flattering — Ember 35.8 at $0.136 per task, Haiku 36.2 at $0.411 per task. The full ladder:

ModelCost per task → Index
Muse Ember$0.116 → 33.9 · $0.136 → 35.8
Claude Haiku 5.5$0.092 → 24.6 · $0.159 → 29.6 · $0.248 → 34.3 · $0.411 → 36.2
GPT-6 Luna$0.073 → 21.4 · $0.104 → 23.3 · $0.152 → 27.8

Read correctly, Meta's claim is not "we beat Haiku." It is "we reach Haiku's best score for about a third of the per-task cost, and we beat every GPT-6 Luna setting outright." Both of those are supported. Note also that Haiku has a four-rung effort ladder in this data and Ember has two — Ember is marked reasoning-capable, but Meta published no effort-setting documentation, so you cannot deliberately pick the $0.116 rung the way you can with Haiku's effort parameter.

Part 4 — The verbosity that eats part of the discount​

One number from the independent index run does not appear in Meta's post: on the Intelligence Index, Ember emitted about 115 million output tokens against a 97.1 million median, with the evaluator describing it as "somewhat verbose."

That is roughly 18% above median output, on a model whose output tokens cost 5x its input tokens. Verbosity is the one place where a cheap executor quietly gives back its advantage, and it compounds with step count: a verbose model writing long intermediate reasoning into a transcript also grows that transcript faster — which pushes the run toward the 200K threshold sooner.

The counter-evidence is real, though, and comes from launch partners rather than Meta: You.com reported a p95 latency of 2.2 seconds and 36–48% fewer output tokens in its own coding tests, and Cloudflare reported it ran up to 30% faster at the tail than the comparable model they tested. Both are partner quotes in a launch post — directionally useful, not independent measurement.

What you'll learn
  • These two findings genuinely conflict: the independent index run measured above-median verbosity, while a launch partner measured 36-48% fewer output tokens than its incumbent. Both can be true — verbosity is workload-dependent, and You.com was comparing against a different baseline than the index median. The resolution is to measure output tokens per completed task on your own workload before you model the savings.

Part 5 — What Meta did not ship​

The gaps are as informative as the specs, and they all point the same direction.

  • No weights. Ember is proprietary and served by Meta. It is not part of Meta's open-weight line.
  • No parameter count, no architecture note, no model card. "Smallest and most efficient" is the entire disclosure.
  • No reasoning-effort ladder. The model is described as reasoning-capable with no published settings, which means you cannot tune the cost/quality rung yourself.
  • No knowledge cutoff, no rate limits, no cache-write price, no batch pricing.
  • No independent throughput numbers. No tokens-per-second, no time-to-first-token, no time-per-task from a third party.
  • Modality is disputed. Meta claims native text, image, audio and video understanding; independent listings at the time of writing record text and image input with text output. If audio or video input is load-bearing for you, verify it on the live model page before building on it.

Safety-wise, Meta says Ember was evaluated under its Superintelligence Scaling Framework across chemical/biological, cybersecurity and loss-of-control risk and stayed below the framework's high-risk thresholds, and that despite its size it received the same core safety training — including testing on resistance to instructions hidden in web pages, documents and tool outputs. That last item is the relevant one for an executor that will spend its life reading untrusted tool output; see prompt injection for why it is the right thing to test and why passing an internal eval is not a guarantee.

Part 6 — Where it fits​

Meta's framing is explicit: Muse Wonder for the hardest problems, Muse Spark for coding and agentic workflows, Muse Ember for repeatable work at scale. For context, Muse Spark 1.3 scores 37 on the index at $2.39 per task on a $1.25/$4.25 rate card — so inside Meta's own lineup, Ember is one index point behind Spark 1.3 at roughly 1/17th the cost per task.

Availability: Muse Code and the Meta Model API, with support for the Responses API; Ember, Spark and Wonder are also offered on Google Cloud and Oracle Cloud. Launch partner Cline described it as purpose-built for subagent work like coding and document analysis.

The honest verdict is the one the price and the benchmark shape both point at: everything about Ember says executor, and nothing published says planner. Put a frontier model on the decisions and Ember on the volume. If you are building that two-tier shape, model routing patterns covers the mechanics, and why agents burn tokens covers where the volume actually comes from.
