AI is a superb research *accelerator* — and a dangerous one if you trust it blindly. Used right, it compresses hours of reading into minutes; used carelessly, it confidently invents sources. Here's the disciplined version.

## High-value research moves

- **Summarize** a long document/paper into key points + implications.
- **Compare** multiple sources into a table (what they agree/disagree on).
- **Build a landscape** — map the players, approaches, or arguments on a topic.
- **Extract** specific facts/figures from a document (with citations).
- **Pressure-test** — "what's the strongest counterargument to this?"

## The grounding discipline (non-negotiable)

<PromptCard title="Grounded summary — quote-per-claim, 'not stated' allowed">{`Using ONLY the text I provide, summarize the key findings and quote the exact
sentence supporting each one. If something isn't in the text, say "not stated."
Text: """[paste]"""`}</PromptCard>

- **Feed it the sources.** Don't ask it to recall facts from memory for anything important — paste the material or use a tool that retrieves it ([RAG](/docs/foundations/rag)).
- **Demand citations.** "Quote the sentence that supports each claim." Unsupported claims become visible.
- **Separate fact from inference.** Ask it to label what's stated vs. interpreted.

The third one is the move most people skip, and it is the cheapest. A summary that mixes "the paper reports X" with "which suggests Y" reads fine and is useless for checking, because you can no longer tell which half you need to verify.

<PromptCard title="Fact vs. inference — force the two apart">{`From the text below, produce two separate lists:
STATED — claims the text makes explicitly, each with the exact supporting sentence.
INFERRED — anything you concluded that the text does not say outright, each with
what it is based on and how confident you are.
Put nothing in STATED that you cannot quote.
Text: """[paste]"""`}</PromptCard>

:::warning Fabricated citations are the classic trap
Models invent realistic-looking papers, authors, and URLs. **Verify every citation exists** before you rely on it. See [Hallucinations](/docs/foundations/hallucinations).
:::

A fabricated citation rarely looks wrong. It looks *exactly* right: a plausible author pair, a real-sounding journal, a year that fits. Which is why the only reliable check is mechanical — resolve the citation, don't evaluate it. Three signals that something needs resolving before you cite it: a DOI or URL that 404s, a title you cannot find anywhere but the model's answer, and a paper quoted for a claim that is slightly *too* perfect a fit for your argument.

## A research flow

<Steps items={[
  {title: "Gather sources yourself", body: "Collect the material, or point a retrieval tool at it. This is the step that decides whether the rest of the work is grounded or improvised."},
  {title: "Summarize each with grounding + citations", body: "One source at a time, with the quote-per-claim prompt above. Summarizing five sources in one prompt is how claims drift between them."},
  {title: "Synthesize across them into a themed comparison", body: "Ask for a table: themes as rows, sources as columns, and an explicit cell for 'does not address this'. The empty cells are often the finding."},
  {title: "Identify gaps and disagreements, then the 'so what'", body: "Where sources disagree, report the disagreement rather than letting the model resolve it silently — a split verdict is information."},
  {title: "Spot-check the critical facts against the originals", body: "Not every fact: the ones your conclusion rests on. If a number would change your recommendation, open the source and read it yourself."}
]} />

<Callout type="tip" items={["Ask for the strongest counterargument in a separate turn, not as part of the summary. Bundled together, the model tends to produce a counterargument it has already defeated.","Keep the source text in the conversation for as long as you are asking questions about it. Once it scrolls out of context, answers quietly shift from reading to remembering.","When comparing sources, give each one a short label (S1, S2, …) and require every claim to carry its label. Unlabeled claims are the model's own voice, and they are now easy to spot."]} />

## Takeaways

<Callout type="takeaways" items={[
  "Feed the sources; never let an important fact come from the model's memory",
  "Demand a supporting quote per claim, and sanction 'not stated' so gaps become visible",
  "Keep STATED and INFERRED in separate lists — mixed, neither can be checked",
  "Resolve citations mechanically. A fabricated one looks exactly right by design",
  "Report disagreement between sources instead of asking the model to settle it",
  "Spot-check the facts your conclusion depends on, in the original"
]} />
