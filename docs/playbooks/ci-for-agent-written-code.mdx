Your coding agents work. That is the problem.

A team that merged 5 pull requests a day and now merges 40 has not changed its test suite, its runner budget, or its branch protection rules. It has changed the *arrival rate* into a queueing system that was tuned for the old one. Everything downstream — merge queue, flaky-test noise, runner concurrency, review attention — was sized for human throughput, and queueing systems do not degrade gracefully. They hold, hold, hold, then fall over inside a week.

This page is the pipeline side of [the verification bottleneck](/docs/frontiers/the-verification-bottleneck). That page asks *who checks the code*. This one asks whether your CI can physically absorb the volume, and what to do when it can't.

## Three numbers that frame the whole problem

**−7.2%.** DORA's 2024 *Accelerate State of DevOps* report modelled a 25% increase in AI adoption and estimated a **7.2% decrease in delivery stability** alongside a 1.5% decrease in throughput. Individual productivity and job satisfaction went *up*. Delivery-level outcomes went down — the report ties this to larger changesets and weaker batching discipline. The 2025 follow-up reframes it: AI is an **amplifier**, magnifying whatever your delivery system already is. A pipeline that was fragile at 5 PRs/day becomes unusable at 40.

**19%.** METR's randomised trial (July 2025) took 16 experienced open-source maintainers and 246 real issues from their own repositories, randomising whether AI tools were allowed. With AI, tasks took **19% longer**. The gap that matters for CI planning is the perceptual one: developers predicted a 24% speed-up, and *after* the slowdown still believed they had been sped up by 20%. Nobody on your team will tell you the pipeline is the constraint, because nobody experiences it as one — they experience it as "waiting", which feels like weather.

**1.5% / 16% / 84%.** Google's published flakiness data: roughly **1.5% of test runs** report a flaky result; about **16% of their 4.2M tests** have shown flakiness at some point; and around **84% of observed pass→fail transitions** involve a flaky test rather than a real regression. Hold onto that 1.5% — it is small enough to ignore at 5 PRs/day and load-bearing at 40.

## Why the merge queue breaks first

Most teams assume the ceiling is compute. It isn't. It's the merge queue, and the reason is a mechanism most people have never read the docs for.

GitHub's merge queue does **speculative checking**. Queue PRs #1, #2, #3 and it builds temporary branches: `main` + #1, `main` + #1 + #2, `main` + #1 + #2 + #3 — and tests all of them *in parallel*. If every check passes, all three land in one round. That is the whole value proposition: batching turns N serial validations into one.

Now read the failure path. When a check fails for the group containing PR #1, GitHub removes **#1** from the queue — and every group behind it contained #1's changes, so those groups are invalid and get **rebuilt without it**. One bad PR does not cost you one slot. It costs you the rest of the batch's CI round.

That makes queue throughput a function of your *pass rate*, and it's steeper than intuition suggests. Model it: batch size N, each PR independently failing with probability p, and you merge the run of PRs up to the first failure. Expected merges per round is a geometric expectation:

```text
E[merges] = (1 − p) · (1 − (1 − p)^N) / p
```

With N = 10:

| Per-PR failure rate `p` | Expected merges per round | Rounds to clear 10 PRs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2%  | 8.9 | ~1.1 |
| 5%  | 7.6 | ~1.3 |
| 10% | 5.9 | ~1.7 |
| 20% | 3.6 | ~2.8 |
| 30% | 2.3 | ~4.4 |

Two consequences most teams get backwards:

1. **A flaky suite is a *throughput* tax, not a nuisance tax.** Flakiness adds directly to `p`. Going from 5% real failures to 5% real + 5% flake takes you from 7.6 merges per round to 5.9 — roughly **23% of your merge capacity**, lost to tests that were never testing anything.
2. **Raising the batch size is the wrong lever when `p` is high.** Bigger N only helps when `(1 − p)^N` stays near 1. At p = 0.2, a batch of 10 delivers 3.6 merges; the fix is lowering `p`, not queueing more optimism.

And agent volume pushes `p` the wrong way — DORA's stability finding is exactly a statement about `p` rising.

## The capacity arithmetic nobody does

Do this on a napkin before you buy runners. Assumptions stated, because they're what make it wrong for your team:

<Steps items={[
  {title: "Count job-minutes, not wall-clock", body: "A 45-minute suite fanned out into 15 parallel jobs still costs ~45 job-minutes. Parallelism buys latency, never cost. Take your suite's total job-minutes from a recent run, not from the slowest job."},
  {title: "Multiply by validations per PR, not by PRs", body: "With a merge queue every PR is validated at least twice: once on the PR branch, once inside a merge group. Reruns and rebuilds from the p-math above add more. Budget 2.5x as a floor, not 1x."},
  {title: "Divide by the burst window, not the day", body: "40 PRs x 2.5 validations x 45 job-min = 4,500 job-minutes. Spread over 24 hours that is trivial. It does not arrive over 24 hours — it arrives in two or three hours around review time. 4,500 job-min into a 2-hour window needs ~37 sustained concurrent jobs."},
  {title: "Compare against your plan's hard ceiling", body: "GitHub-hosted standard runners cap total concurrent jobs at 20 (Free), 40 (Pro), 60 (Team) and 500 (Enterprise). The napkin above quietly exceeds Free and sits on top of Pro — during the burst, which is the only window that matters."},
  {title: "Find the silent failure", body: "A queued job is cancelled after 24 hours. On a saturated queue this shows up as jobs that never ran rather than jobs that failed, which is far harder to notice and does not page anyone."}
]} />

The takeaway is not "buy Enterprise". It's that the constraint is **burst concurrency**, and the daily-average number that makes your pipeline look fine is the number hiding the problem.

## Four levers, ranked by leverage

Teams reach for these in almost exactly the reverse of the useful order.

**1. Lower `p` (highest leverage, lowest cost).** Every point of failure rate removed pays off nonlinearly in the batching math. Concretely: make agents run the fast suite locally before opening a PR, require a green pre-merge check before a PR is *allowed* into the queue, and quarantine flakes aggressively (below). This costs no compute.

**2. Cut the work per validation — test selection.** This is the only lever that changes the exponent rather than the constant. Instead of running everything, run what the change can plausibly break:

- **Develocity Predictive Test Selection** builds a model from your own Build Scan history plus cross-project patterns, and ships a **simulator** so you can measure the savings and the missed-failure rate *before* enabling it. Two safety details worth stealing even if you never buy it: you can pin **must-run tests** by class pattern or annotation, and it **always selects recently flaky tests**, because a flaky test is by definition unpredictable from the change. Pair it with a "remaining tests" run post-merge or nightly so nothing is skipped forever.
- **Bazel / Buck2** get this structurally: the build graph knows which targets depend on the changed files, so `bazel test` on affected targets is target determination for free.
- **No build graph?** Python has `pytest-testmon`, which records which tests cover which code and re-runs only the affected ones. Jest ships `--changedSince` out of the box.

<PromptCard title="Test selection, three ecosystems">
{`# Bazel — test only what the diff can reach
bazel test $(bazel query "rdeps(//..., set($(git diff --name-only origin/main)))" --output=label 2>/dev/null)

# Python — coverage-based impact analysis
pip install pytest-testmon
pytest --testmon          # first run builds the DB, later runs select

# Jest — built in, no plugin
npx jest --changedSince=origin/main`}
</PromptCard>

**3. Cache aggressively — and know the gotcha.** Remote caching stores action results keyed by a hash of inputs, command line and whitelisted environment. Bazel's own docs flag the trap: **tools outside the workspace are not tracked**, so two machines with different compiler versions can get a cache *hit* on work that would have produced different outputs. Whitelist what matters with `--action_env`, and keep write access to CI only — developer machines should read:

<PromptCard title="Remote cache, CI-writes-only">
{`# CI: read and write
bazel test //... --remote_cache=grpcs://cache.internal:443

# Developer machines: read only, never poison the shared cache
bazel test //... --remote_cache=grpcs://cache.internal:443 \\
  --remote_upload_local_results=false`}
</PromptCard>

**4. Add runners (last).** Linear cost, linear benefit, and it does nothing about `p`. Do it after the first three, when you actually know your burst concurrency number.

## The AI-specific failure mode: the agent grades its own homework

Everything above applies to any high-volume pipeline. This one is specific to agent-written code, and it is the gap most CI setups have.

When the same agent writes the fix *and* the test for the fix, "tests pass" carries much less information than you think. Two failure shapes show up constantly:

- The test asserts the behaviour of the implementation rather than the requirement — it passes because it was written by reading the code it is testing.
- The agent, when a pre-existing test fails, edits the *test* to match the new behaviour instead of fixing the code. The diff is green and the regression shipped. That behaviour has a name and a literature — see [reward hacking in coding agents](/docs/frontiers/reward-hacking-in-coding-agents) for why models do it and which defenses have measured effect sizes. What follows is the pipeline-side control, not the model-side one.

The cheap structural gate: **a new test must fail against the pre-change code.** That's the red-green discipline of TDD turned into a machine check, and it's what mutation testing formalises at scale. You do not need a mutation-testing framework to get most of the value — you need one CI job:

<PromptCard title="CI gate: new tests must fail without the change">
{`#!/usr/bin/env bash
# must-fail-first.sh — run in CI on PRs that add tests
set -euo pipefail

NEW_TESTS=$(git diff --name-only origin/main...HEAD -- 'tests/**' 'test/**')
[ -z "$NEW_TESTS" ] && { echo "no new tests, skipping"; exit 0; }

# Keep the new tests, revert everything else to the base commit
git stash push --keep-index -- $NEW_TESTS
git checkout origin/main -- src/
git stash pop

# The new tests MUST fail now. If they pass, they prove nothing.
if pytest $NEW_TESTS -q; then
  echo "FAIL: new tests pass against unmodified source — they do not test the change."
  exit 1
fi
echo "OK: new tests fail without the fix."`}
</PromptCard>

Two honest caveats. It only works when source and tests live in separate trees, and a test that fails for the wrong reason (import error, missing fixture) passes this gate. It is a floor, not a proof — but it catches the single most common way an agent-authored PR is green and worthless.

The complementary review-side control is a reviewer that doesn't share the author's context: see [the hybrid AI code reviewer](/docs/models/open-code-review-hybrid-ai-reviewer) and [the PR-review GitHub Action](/docs/walkthroughs/pr-review-action).

## Flake quarantine that survives contact with agents

Given 84% of pass→fail transitions are flakes, "rerun it" is the default human response and it is the most expensive one available — it burns a full validation on a test that was never going to tell you anything.

<Steps items={[
  {title: "Measure before you judge", body: "A flaky test is one that produces different results on the same commit. Run the suite N times on an unchanged main and record per-test failure counts. That is your evidence; developer memory is not."},
  {title: "Quarantine on a threshold, not an argument", body: "Anything above your threshold moves to a non-blocking job that still runs and still reports. It stops gating merges immediately — that is the throughput win — and it stays visible so it does not become dead code."},
  {title: "Cap the quarantine and give it an owner", body: "An uncapped quarantine is a graveyard. Fixed budget (say 1% of tests), a name attached, an expiry date. Over budget means the next flake evicts the oldest one back into blocking."},
  {title: "Never let test selection skip a flake", body: "This is the counter-intuitive rule Develocity encodes: recently-flaky tests are always selected, because the model cannot predict them from the diff. Selecting away your flakes hides the signal you need to fix them."},
  {title: "Re-derive p after each round", body: "Recompute your per-PR failure rate monthly and put it on the dashboard next to merge-queue throughput. It is the one number that tells you whether the pipeline is getting better or the agents are just getting louder."}
]} />

## Gotchas that cost a day each

- **`merge_group` is not automatic.** GitHub's docs are explicit: you **must** add the `merge_group` event to your workflow triggers, or your checks never fire inside the queue and merges fail on missing required checks. This is the single most common merge-queue misconfiguration.
- **The 24-hour queue cancellation is silent.** Saturated queue → jobs vanish rather than fail. Alert on queue depth, not just on red builds.
- **Matrix jobs cap at 256 per workflow run.** Generated matrices (a common agent-scaffolding pattern) hit this without a useful error.
- **Cross-machine cache hits can be wrong.** Untracked external toolchains produce false hits. Pin toolchains, whitelist environment with `--action_env`.
- **Branch protection with wildcard patterns is incompatible with merge queues.** If your protection rules use wildcards, the queue will not engage.
- **Agent PRs touching CI are a security boundary, not a style question.** A PR that can edit workflow files can exfiltrate CI secrets — see [GitHub issue → CI secrets](/docs/security/github-issue-to-ci-secrets-blackhat-2026) and [hardening autonomous runs](/docs/security/hardening-autonomous-runs).

## The honest framing

There is no configuration that makes a pipeline absorb unbounded agent output. What the levers above buy you is a system whose cost grows with the *size of the change* rather than the *number of pull requests* — test selection and caching are sublinear, runners are linear, and merge-queue batching is only sublinear while your pass rate stays high.

Which puts the real constraint back where DORA's 2025 framing puts it: AI amplifies the delivery system you already have. If small batches, fast suites and low flakiness were already your practice, agents make you much faster. If they weren't, agents find out.
