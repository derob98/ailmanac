## The study, in one paragraph

Nine researchers, mostly at [IMDEA Networks](https://networks.imdea.org/) in Madrid, took the nine
most-used conversational AI services and instrumented them the way the mobile-tracking
research community instruments any app: Chrome v148 driven through the Chrome DevTools
Protocol with full HAR capture for the web clients, and a Google Pixel 3a running an
instrumented Android 12 AOSP build with `mitmproxy` and Frida for the eight that ship an
Android app — plus static decompilation with Androguard to enumerate embedded SDKs. Every
session was driven by hand with a fixed, deliberately sensitive persona script (a user asking
about a medical condition), repeated across guest / free / paid tiers and across three consent
states: ignore the banner, reject non-essential cookies, accept everything. Measurements were
taken in Spain in May 2026. The paper was accepted at PoPETs — the peer-reviewed venue for
privacy technology — and the authors ran a responsible-disclosure process with the providers
and with European data protection authorities before publishing.

That methodology matters because it is the difference between "someone found a Meta pixel on
a page" and a defensible claim about what is transmitted, under which consent state, on which
tier, to whom.

Third-party integration is not the same set on web and on mobile: 11 tracking organisations
appear on both client types, 15 are web-only (including Google Tag Manager, TikTok and the
consent-management platform OneTrust), and 8 are mobile-only (including Braze). Auditing the
website therefore tells you very little about the app, and vice versa.

## Why conversation artifacts are a different problem

Ordinary web tracking observes *behaviour*: which page you loaded, how long you stayed, what
you clicked. Conversational AI generates a new class of object — the provider itself
manufactures a short, human-readable summary of what you are thinking about, and then that
object travels through the same pipes.

The paper's Appendix A shows exactly what that summary looks like. Same prompt, three
providers:

| Prompt typed by the user | Title generated by ChatGPT | by Claude | by Grok |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| "What are the symptoms of early-stage Parkinson's disease?" | Early-stage Parkinson's Symptoms | Early-stage Parkinson's disease symptoms | Early Parkinson's Disease Symptoms |
| "My salary is $85k. How much mortgage can I afford in NYC?" | Mortgage Affordability in NYC | Mortgage affordability on $85k salary in NYC | $85k NYC Salary: $280k-$350k Mortgage |

Look at the bottom-right cell. Grok's title does not merely record the topic — it encodes the
answer, including a salary figure and a mortgage range, into a short string. When that string
is transmitted to an ad network alongside a stable identifier, the ad network has learned a
health condition or a financial profile from a single field. No inference, no modelling, no
clickstream reconstruction. The provider did the summarisation for them.

Here is what the paper counted leaking, and to how many distinct tracking organisations:

| Artifact leaked | Providers (web) | Providers (Android) | Trackers (web) | Trackers (Android) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Conversation URL | 5 | 0 | 9 | 0 |
| Conversation ID | 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| User prompt | 1 | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| Conversation title | 3 | 0 | 9 | 0 |
| Conversation screenshot | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| Shared conversation URL | 5 | 0 | 9 | 0 |
| Shared conversation ID | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |

The asymmetry is worth internalising: the *web* clients leak the user-facing artifacts (titles,
URLs, screenshots) because web trackers execute inside the page's JavaScript context and can
simply read the DOM. Mobile SDKs run outside the WebView's document, so they mostly get the
conversation ID — but in exchange they get a much richer identifier set, because a third-party
SDK inherits the host app's permissions.

## Three mechanisms that make your ad blocker irrelevant

This is the part most coverage misses, and it is the most transferable lesson on the page.
Blocking `doubleclick.net` in your browser does nothing against any of the following.

<Steps items={[
  {"title": "Server-side tag managers (measured on Grok)", "body": "Grok's web client loads a Google Tag Manager container configured to route events through a server-side GTM instance (sGTM). A custom event forwards the conversation URL and the chat topic server-to-server to Meta's Conversions API and TikTok's Events API.   {"title": "First-party-proxied analytics (measured on Claude)", "body": "Claude's web client loads Segment Analytics proxied through a first-party domain, a-cdn.anthropic.com, carrying a Conversion API configuration that forwards user events server-to-server from Anthropic's infrastructure to eleven downstream trackers — the paper names Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok, Reddit and Google Enhanced Conversions among them — with two shared IDs per event. ## The consent numbers, read honestly

This is where the study is most useful and most depressing, because it quantifies something
everyone suspects.

<Callout type="note" items={[
  "77.3% of observed transmissions of web identifiers to third parties occur only after the user accepts non-essential cookies. Your choice does something.",
  "But 80.8% of third-party trackers remain active even when non-essential cookies are rejected. What your choice removes is the minority.",
  "Across all analysed free-tier services, third-party trackers still collect data in 44.4% (4 of 9) of them under a reject-all configuration.",
  "Of the three web clients that leak conversation titles, 88.9% of those disclosures happen only on accept — titles are among the artifacts consent actually gates.",
  "Ignoring the banner entirely produced no connections beyond those observed under explicit rejection. Clicking the X is, on these nine services, equivalent to rejecting.",
  "Subscription tier barely matters. Free and premium accounts contacted largely identical third parties; Claude's mobile free tier was the single clear exception.",
]} />

Two structural observations follow, and they are the ones to remember:

**The strongest controls are least available to the users most exposed.** Persistent consent
preferences on the web exist only on ChatGPT's paid tier, while roughly 95% of ChatGPT users sit
on free tiers — and OpenAI has announced advertising for free and low-cost users. On mobile,
most apps present no cookie-consent equivalent at launch at all, despite exposing similar
tracking infrastructure. You cannot reject what you were never asked about.

**Presence is not proof of purpose.** The paper is careful here and so should you be: a third
party appearing in traffic does not by itself mean data is being used for advertising. Some SDKs
do crash reporting, session replay or in-app payments. Sentry, Datadog and Intercom have obvious
operational justifications. The authors also note their methodology cannot separate a provider's
own first-party server-side tracking from functional traffic. Read the tables as "these entities
receive this data", not "this data is definitely sold".

## Permalinks are not an access control

Every one of the nine services lets you share a conversation, and every one of them serves the
shared conversation without requiring the recipient to authenticate. The security model is the
unguessability of a UUID in the URL. Table 6 in the paper measures the default posture per
provider and tier; the two outliers are Grok, where free and premium conversation permalinks are
public by default with an opt-out, and Perplexity, where guest-tier conversations are always
public with no opt-out at all. Perplexity is also the only provider that offers a whitelist
control on top.

The authors then did the thing that turns a theoretical exposure into a measured one. They
deployed canary tokens — from `canarytokens.org` and from a self-hosted canary infrastructure
with a generic domain, because some assistants recognise and refuse known canary domains — both
pasted directly into prompts and embedded inside uploaded `.docx` and `.pdf` files. Then they
waited to see who fetched them.

<Callout type="warning" items={[
  "For DeepSeek, Copilot, Mistral and Claude, some canary URLs fired once at submission time, from AWS or GCP addresses — consistent with the provider's own link-preview or retrieval infrastructure.",
  "For Grok, they recorded 70 distinct canary activations over hours to days after submission, from 70 IP addresses spanning 48 autonomous systems in 14 countries across 4 continents, with 65.7% of connections originating in the United States.",
  "Perplexity repeatedly fetched canary URLs even when the prompt explicitly instructed it not to, from IP ranges belonging to its Perplexity-User crawler on AWS infrastructure in the US."
]} />

Seventy retrievals from forty-eight different networks, days later, is not link-preview
behaviour. It is the signature of a URL that escaped into a pipeline and got crawled. The authors
are appropriately cautious — absence of observed access elsewhere is not evidence of absence,
since they only see what their own instrumentation catches — but the positive result stands on
its own.

Grok was also the only service where the authors identified something they classified as a
security concern exploitable by an external party, rather than an intentional product decision.
That is why xAI got a vulnerability-disclosure email on 2026-04-17 rather than an ordinary
research notification. As of 2026-09-10 the paper reports that Grok still uses publicly
accessible permalinks and that no official response had been received.

## Run the audit yourself

None of this requires research infrastructure. You can reproduce the shape of the finding on any
assistant in about twenty minutes, and you should, because the stacks change constantly and the
paper is explicitly a point-in-time lower bound.

<PromptCard title="Extract every third-party host and hunt for your own identifiers in a HAR">
{`# Every distinct host the page contacted, by request count
jq -r '.log.entries[].request.url' capture.har \\
  | awk -F/ '{print $3}' | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn

# Any request whose URL or POST body contains the conversation UUID
CONV_ID="paste-the-uuid-from-the-address-bar"
jq -r --arg id "$CONV_ID" '
  .log.entries[]
  | select((.request.url | contains($id))
        or ((.request.postData.text // "") | contains($id)))
  | "\\(.request.method) \\(.request.url)"' capture.har

# Your email, in clear and in the three hashes trackers actually use
EMAIL="you@example.com"
for h in "$EMAIL" \\
         "$(printf %s "$EMAIL" | md5)" \\
         "$(printf %s "$EMAIL" | shasum -a 1   | cut -d' ' -f1)" \\
         "$(printf %s "$EMAIL" | shasum -a 256 | cut -d' ' -f1)"; do
  printf '%s -> ' "$h"
  grep -c -- "$h" capture.har
done`}

## If you build on these APIs, this is your problem too

The paper's scope note is easy to skim past and it is the part that should worry engineers: the
leakage vectors observed here "stem from embedding standard web and mobile tracking directly into
conversational interfaces". Nothing about them is specific to a frontier lab. If you have shipped
an LLM-powered support chatbot, a custom assistant or a third-party AI wrapper, and your marketing
team has installed the usual analytics on the same pages, you have almost certainly reproduced
this architecture by accident.

Three concrete things to check in your own product:

- **Do you generate conversation titles or summaries client-side?** If so, they are in the DOM,
  and any tag manager on that page can read them. The fix is to keep generated summaries out of
  the document title, out of `<meta>` tags, and out of any analytics event payload.
- **Are conversation IDs in the URL path?** Then they are in the `Referer` header of every
  third-party request the page makes, and in `document.location` for every script. The study found
  two providers shipping the global conversation ID to Datadog as an independent parameter, which
  is enough to reconstruct a permalink.
- **Is your share page publicly readable?** Under the ePrivacy Directive's Article 5(3) and the
  EDPB's Guidelines 2/2023 on its technical scope, URL and pixel tracking, persistent identifiers,
  and instructing the browser to forward data to a server-side API all require prior informed
  consent. The CJEU's *Fashion ID* judgment (C-40/17, paragraph 78) goes further: enabling
  third-party access is itself a processing decision that triggers your obligations, whether or not
  the third party ever reads the data.
