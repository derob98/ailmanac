Between August and early October 2026, three conversations with a commercial AI
assistant publicly became police matters. One produced a felony arrest. The
pattern is not a glitch and it is not new policy — it is the documented
operation of an abuse pipeline every major provider runs, described in help-centre
articles that almost nobody reads before pasting.

The useful question is not *"do they read my chats?"* — the answer has been yes,
in a narrow and specific sense, for years. The useful questions are **what
triggers a human to look**, **what a human is authorised to do next**, and
**which of your privacy settings touch that path at all.** The third answer is
the one that surprises people: mostly none of them.

## What actually happened in the three cases

<VerifyNote lastVerified="2026-10-06" source="https://sfstandard.com/2026/09/04/anthropic-threat-claude-sfpd/">
Case facts are from contemporaneous reporting and are the subject of pending
criminal proceedings; charges are allegations, not findings. Policy figures are
from provider documentation current on the verify date and change without notice
— re-read the linked help-centre articles before relying on any number here.
</VerifyNote>

**San Francisco, August 2026.** A Claude user allegedly wrote that he had bought
an AR-15 and had Anthropic's CEO "in his sights." Per the police report cited by
*The San Francisco Standard*, the messages were sent on Friday 14 August at
2:30 p.m. Anthropic notified SFPD the **following Tuesday at 9 a.m.** Officers
attended Anthropic's office; the subject was not present and, as of that
reporting, had not been arrested or charged. He told the *Standard* he had not
been serious. Anthropic's statement: the account was banned and the case
referred, and "this is our safeguards process working as intended."

**Florida, late September 2026.** Carli Michelle Heller of Bonita Springs had
been using Claude much as one uses a journal. According to the arrest report, on
26 September she wrote that she would shoot up the Lee County Sheriff's Office,
and on 27 September that she had obtained a new gun. Automated systems flagged
the text, a human review team assessed it, Anthropic reported it, deputies
located her at home and detained her without incident. She was charged with
making a written threat of violence — a second-degree felony — with a court date
in November.

*Tom's Hardware* counted the Florida case as **at least the third** Claude
conversation to reach police since August.

### The three details worth extracting

1. **The latency is days, not seconds.** Friday afternoon to Tuesday morning is
   roughly four days. This is a review queue staffed by people, not a tripwire
   wired to a dispatcher. That cuts both ways: it is not real-time surveillance,
   and it is also not a system that can intervene in a genuinely imminent event.
2. **A human made the call.** In both cases the automated classifier only
   *routed*. The decision to contact police was made by a trained reviewer
   reading the text. The thing people find most uncomfortable — an employee read
   my private journal — is the deliberate design, not the failure mode.
3. **Account termination came first.** Banning preceded or accompanied referral.
   If you are ever on the wrong side of a classifier for innocent reasons, the
   first symptom is losing access, not a phone call.

### The asymmetry almost nobody quotes

When OpenAI published the passage above, the sentence that followed it got far
less attention and is arguably more consequential:

> OpenAI stated it is "currently not referring self-harm cases to law
> enforcement to respect people's privacy given the uniquely private nature of
> ChatGPT interactions."

So the published line is drawn at **harm to others**, not at distress. Someone
in crisis about themselves is handled inside the product — crisis resources,
model behaviour changes, possibly human review — without a police referral.
Someone who writes a threat against a third party is in a different pipeline
entirely. If you only remember one thing about escalation thresholds, remember
that the line is about *who gets hurt*, and that it is a policy choice a
provider can revise.

## The retention arithmetic

This is the section that changes behaviour, because the numbers are longer than
almost anyone assumes and several of them are immune to the controls in your
settings panel.

| What | Provider | Duration | Survives you deleting it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal conversation you delete | Anthropic (consumer) | Gone from history immediately, purged from back-end storage within **30 days** | — |
| Conversation **flagged** as violating the Usage Policy | Anthropic | Inputs and outputs up to **2 years** | **Yes** |
| Trust-and-safety **classification scores** for that conversation | Anthropic | Up to **7 years** | **Yes** |
| Data you opted into model improvement | Anthropic (consumer) | De-identified, up to **5 years** in training pipelines | Per opt-out at the time |
| Chat read by a **human reviewer** | Google (Gemini Apps) | Up to **3 years**, disconnected from your Google Account | **Yes** — deleting your activity does not remove it |
| Gemini Apps Activity **on** | Google | Auto-delete at **18 months** by default (adjustable to 3 or 36 months, or off) | — |
| Gemini Apps Activity **off** | Google | Temporary chats kept **72 hours** to respond to you and protect safety | — |
| ChatGPT **Temporary Chat** | OpenAI | Not in history, not in memory, not used for training; may be retained up to **30 days** for safety | — |
| Prompts and completions on a **Covered Model** | Anthropic | **At least 30 days**, mandatory, then auto-deleted | — |

Two rows deserve to be read twice.

**Seven years of classification scores.** The conversation itself may be gone;
the structured judgement about it persists for the better part of a decade. A
classification score is not a copy of your text, but it is a durable,
account-linked assertion that your text scored a certain way on a certain
category. For anyone reasoning about long-term exposure, that is the real
retention horizon, and it is roughly 85× longer than the 30-day purge window
people cite when they say deletion works.

**Three years that outlive deletion.** Google is explicit that human-reviewed
chats and their associated metadata — language, device type, location
information, feedback — are *not* deleted when you delete your activity,
precisely because they have been disconnected from your account. Anonymisation
is what makes the retention defensible and also what makes it permanent from
your side: there is no longer an account handle to issue the delete against.
Google tries to disconnect reviewed chats before a human sees them, but anything
you typed *into* the message is part of the message.

## The ZDR myth

This is the single most common misconception among developers who believe they
have solved the problem contractually.

Zero Data Retention for Claude Code on Claude for Enterprise means prompts and
responses are processed in real time and "not stored by Anthropic after the
response is returned" — **"except where needed to comply with law or combat
misuse."** Anthropic's own documentation spells the exception out:

> Even with ZDR enabled, Anthropic may retain data where required by law or to
> address Usage Policy violations. If a session is flagged for a policy
> violation, Anthropic may retain the associated inputs and outputs for up to
> 2 years.

And separately, on what ZDR never covered in the first place:

> Anthropic still retains User Safety classifier results in order to enforce
> our Usage Policy.

So the correct mental model is: **ZDR removes the storage, not the pipeline.**
Classification still happens on every request; the scores are still kept; a
flagged session still gets a two-year retention. ZDR is an excellent control
against *routine* retention and a poor one against *abuse-response* retention,
which is exactly the retention that matters if something you sent is ever
contested.

### Four more things ZDR does not do

Anthropic documents these explicitly, and each has bitten someone:

- **It does not cover the web app or Cowork.** ZDR for Claude Code covers Claude
  Code inference. Chat on claude.ai through the same Enterprise interface, and
  Cowork sessions, follow standard retention.
- **It does not follow the user, only the organisation.** ZDR applies to requests
  that authenticate into a ZDR-enabled org. A developer who signs in with a
  personal account, or with an API key from a different org, is simply not
  covered. Anthropic's answer is the `forceLoginMethod` and `forceLoginOrgUUID`
  managed settings — worth deploying if you believe ZDR is load-bearing for you.
- **It does not propagate to new organisations.** ZDR is enabled per-org by
  Anthropic's account team and does not automatically apply to new orgs created
  under the same account.
- **It does not cover third parties.** Data processed by MCP servers and external
  integrations is outside ZDR entirely. (See [What Your Agent
  Uploads](/docs/security/what-your-agent-uploads) and [Securing MCP
  Servers](/docs/security/securing-mcp-servers).)

### Covered Models: the capability/retention trade is now explicit

Anthropic designates its most capable models — currently Claude Mythos 5.1,
Claude Fable 5.1, Claude Mythos 5 and Claude Fable 5 — as **Covered Models**,
which require data retention by default. Prompts and completions are retained
for at least 30 days, then automatically deleted, and the retained window is
assessed by automated safety systems designed to flag harmful content.

The stated reason is the genuinely interesting part, because it is an admission
about the limits of per-request filtering:

> Because "some forms of misuse only become detectable across many requests,
> these models require safeguards that operate over a retained window of usage
> data rather than on a single request at a time."

A single request that assembles one benign-looking piece of a dangerous whole is
invisible to a stateless classifier. Detecting the pattern requires remembering
the requests. That is a real security argument, and it means **capability and
retention are now coupled by design**: for these models, you can have the
frontier capability or you can have zero retention, not both. ZDR is unavailable
for Covered Models in standard configurations; where an org or workspace lacks
the mandatory retention, requests for those models are rejected server-side
regardless of client configuration, and Claude Code's `best` alias quietly
resolves to Opus instead of Fable. Anthropic has signalled an Enterprise
Frontier Safeguards path for eligible customers to use Fable 5 and 5.1 under ZDR
for internal business applications, rolling out in autumn 2026 — confirm current
status with your account team rather than assuming either way.

For the model-side view of this, see the [Fable 5.1 Field
Guide](/docs/models/fable-5-1-field-guide), which notes the same mandatory
30-day window from the API-behaviour angle: a request from an org without it
returns a 400.

## What your privacy settings actually control

Here is the clean separation, and it is the practical thesis of this page.

| Control | Governs | Does **not** govern |
|---|---|---|
| Anthropic incognito chat | Excluded from model improvement even when improvement is on | Safety flagging and the retention that follows it |
| ChatGPT Temporary Chat | History, memory, training | Safety/abuse retention — up to 30 days per OpenAI's FAQ |
| Gemini Apps Activity off | Training, human review of future chats, long-term activity storage | A 72-hour window kept to respond to you *and protect safety* |
| Delete conversation | Your history, and back-end copies on a purge timer | Flagged content, classification scores, already-reviewed chats |
| Zero Data Retention | Routine storage of prompts and completions | Classification itself, score retention, flagged-session retention |

Read the right-hand column as a single sentence: **consumer privacy controls are
training-and-history controls. None of them is an abuse-monitoring control.**
That is not a gotcha hidden in fine print — it is the only way a usage policy can
be enforceable at all. But it does mean the mental model "incognito means nobody
sees this" is wrong in a way that matters.

One control genuinely does narrow human review: Google lets you turn off the
pathway that feeds chats to reviewers, via **myaccount.google.com → Data and
Privacy → Gemini Apps Activity → Turn off**. Future conversations are then not
sent for human review and not used for training. Chats already reviewed are
still inside their three-year window.

## The legal mechanism, briefly

Heller was charged under Florida's written-threats statute, **Fla. Stat.
836.10**. Its elements are worth reading because they explain how writing to a
chatbot can be charged at all. The statute reaches a person who sends, posts or
transmits — or procures the sending, posting or transmission of — a writing or
other record, **including an electronic record**, *"in any manner in which it may
be viewed by another person,"* containing a threat to kill or do bodily harm, or
to conduct a mass shooting or an act of terrorism. It is a second-degree felony.

Two observations, offered as reading of the statutory text rather than as legal
advice:

- **The text does not require an intended human recipient.** It requires a record
  transmitted in a manner in which it *may be viewed* by another person. A
  conversation stored on a provider's servers and readable by that provider's
  reviewers is not obviously outside that language. This is precisely the gap
  between what a user feels they are doing (writing privately) and what the
  statute describes (transmitting a viewable electronic record).
- **Intent is still an element.** Florida pattern elements require that the
  defendant intended the threat as a *true threat* — the doctrinal line that
  separates a prosecutable threat from hyperbole or venting, and the obvious
  terrain for a defence. "I was just venting to a chatbot" is a factual argument
  about intent, not a safe harbour created by the medium.

If you need the statute itself, it is published by the Florida Senate; the link
is in the sources. If you are in actual legal trouble, talk to a lawyer, not a
field guide.

## So where should text go?

The honest decision procedure, by class of text.

| Class of text | Right surface | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary work, code, writing | Any hosted assistant | The pipeline exists but is irrelevant to you |
| Confidential business data | Commercial/Enterprise tier with the right terms; ZDR if you qualify | Routine retention removed; abuse retention remains |
| Regulated data (health, financial) | BAA/HIPAA-configured enterprise deployment | Contractual, not just technical, protection |
| Security research touching violence, weapons, exploits | Hosted is fine — but **supply context in-band** | Reviewers read text, and context is what distinguishes research from intent |
| Journalling, crisis, intrusive thoughts, ideation | **Local model, or a human** | The one class where the pipeline is genuinely adverse to the user's interest |

That last row is the Florida case in one line. A chat assistant has the
*affordance* of a diary — private-feeling, always available, never bored — and
none of its *properties*. If you want the affordance, run the model on your own
machine, where the pipeline does not exist because there is no server.

<PromptCard title="A journal that no classifier ever sees">{`# one-time: install Ollama, then pull a small local model
ollama pull llama3.2

# a private journalling session — no network, no review queue, no retention
ollama run llama3.2`}</PromptCard>

Everything in that session stays on your disk. See [Run Models Locally with

The other practical move: when your legitimate work genuinely does involve
violence, weapons, self-harm or exploitation as *subject matter*, say so in the
conversation. A classifier scores text; a human reviewer reads it. Stated context
is the only thing in your control that changes what the reviewer concludes.

<PromptCard title="Give the reviewer the context a classifier cannot infer">{`Context for this conversation, stated up front:

I am a [threat analyst / security researcher / novelist / clinician / trust-and-safety
engineer] working on [specific artefact: a tabletop exercise, a published paper,
chapter 14 of a manuscript, an abuse-detection ruleset].

I need to discuss [violent / self-harm / weapons / exploitation] subject matter as
material to be analysed, depicted or detected — not planned. My output is
[a report / fiction / a detection rule], not an operational plan.

Please keep that frame for the whole thread, and tell me if a request of mine
crosses from analysis into operational detail so I can rephrase it.`}</PromptCard>

That last line is the useful half. It asks the model to act as a cooperative
boundary-keeper rather than leaving you to guess, and a thread containing an
explicit refusal-and-rephrase exchange reads very differently to a reviewer than
one that does not.

## What this does not mean

Three over-corrections to avoid, because the discourse around these cases
produced all three:

- **It is not continuous surveillance by humans.** The human step is reached by a
  small fraction of conversations routed by classifier, in specific categories.
  The default experience of every one of these pipelines is that nobody ever
  reads your chat.
- **It is not new.** Content classification plus a human abuse queue plus a
  narrow legal-referral clause is the standard architecture of every large
  communications platform, and has been for two decades. What is new is the
  *intimacy* of the input — people tell an assistant things they would never post
   — and the fact that the retention exceptions are now long and written down.
- **It is not unique to one provider.** Every table in this page has entries for
  Anthropic, OpenAI and Google because all three publish the same basic design.
  Choosing a provider on this axis means comparing specific documented numbers,
  not vibes. The one real axis of choice is **hosted versus local**, and
  [Choosing a Model Provider](/docs/foundations/choosing-a-model-provider) is the
  page for the rest of that decision.
