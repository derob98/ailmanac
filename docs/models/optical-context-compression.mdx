<LevelBadge level="advanced" />

Every long-context trick of the last two years tried to make the *tokenizer* cheaper: better chunking, better retrieval, sparser attention. A different line of work asks a stranger question — **what if you stop tokenizing the text at all, render it into a picture, and let the vision encoder do the compressing?**

It sounds like a joke. It isn't. Apple's [LensVLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07019) hit the Hacker News front page this week doing exactly this, and it sits on top of a year of results — DeepSeek-OCR, Tsinghua/Zhipu's Glyph — showing that a page of text costs **far fewer tokens as pixels than as characters**, right up until it suddenly doesn't.

This page covers the mechanism (it's simpler than you'd guess), the numbers across Claude, GPT and Gemini (they disagree violently), the accuracy cliff that everyone hits, and a 15-minute experiment to find where that cliff is on *your* documents.

## The mechanism: resolution is the dial a tokenizer never had

A text tokenizer gives you a roughly fixed exchange rate. English prose lands near four characters per token, source code somewhat worse, and there is **no setting** that changes it. You can delete text or you can pay for it.

A vision encoder works the opposite way. It maps an image of a given size to a **fixed number of visual tokens**, regardless of how much information you crammed into those pixels. Render 40 lines of text into a 1000x1000 image and you pay for a 1000x1000 image. Render 400 lines into the same 1000x1000 image, at a tenth of the font size, and you pay **exactly the same**.

That is the whole idea. Rendering resolution becomes a continuous compression knob — and unlike chunking or summarization, it is *lossy in a visual way*: nothing is removed, everything just gets smaller.

Each provider turns pixels into tokens differently, and the differences matter more than the marketing:

| Provider | How images are counted | Practical consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Claude** | 28x28-pixel patches: an image costs `ceil(width/28) x ceil(height/28)` visual tokens | Cheap per pixel, and hard-capped (see below) |
| **OpenAI** | 32x32-pixel patches, multiplied by a per-model multiplier; high-detail mode allows up to 2500 patches | A 1024x1024 image is 1024 patches, billed as `ceil(1024 x 1.2)` = 1229 tokens on a 1.2x-multiplier model |
| **Gemini** | Any image with both dimensions ≤ 384 px costs a flat **258 tokens**; bigger images are cut into 768x768 tiles at **258 tokens each** | There is a hard **floor**. A postage-stamp image of three lines of code still costs 258 tokens |

## The measured numbers, and the one that goes the wrong way

In July 2026 a cross-provider study ([arXiv 2607.21672](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21672)) did the boring, necessary work: it took real source code — five languages, nine lengths from 20 to 2,000 lines, 15 model aliases, 675 matched text/image pairs — and asked each provider's own API how many input tokens it counted, as text and as a rendered image.

Aggregate image-to-text token ratios:

| Provider | Image / text token ratio | Reduction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anthropic | **0.135** | 86.5% fewer counted tokens |
| OpenAI | **0.194** | 80.6% fewer |
| Google (Vertex AI) | **0.242** | 75.8% fewer |

So far, so uniformly good. The interesting result is the **shape**, not the average. On Anthropic and OpenAI, images counted lower **at every tested size** — even 20 lines. On Gemini, an image of 20 lines of code cost **6.95x as many tokens as the text**, and images only dropped below text at around **200 lines**.

That is the 258-token floor doing its work. Below roughly 200 lines, you are buying a whole tile to hold a handful of glyphs.

  "Provider ratios are not transferable. The same pipeline that saves 86% on Claude can cost you 7x on Gemini for short inputs."
]} />

## The non-obvious part: the cap is doing most of the work

Here is what makes those aggregate ratios look so spectacular on long files, and why they are a trap.

Claude does not let you buy an arbitrarily large image. Each model sits in a resolution tier with a **long-edge limit and a visual-token limit**, and anything larger is **downscaled before processing**:

| Tier | Models | Max long edge | Max visual tokens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High-resolution | Claude 4.7 and later | 2576 px | 4784 |
| Standard | All other models | 1568 px | 1568 |

Read that as a price ceiling. On a standard-tier model, one image costs **at most 1568 visual tokens** — whether it contains 200 lines of code or 2,000. Pour in more text and the bill does not move.

Neither does the pixel budget. The glyphs just get smaller.

This is exactly the failure mode the LensVLM authors name: accuracy *"deteriorates quickly as compression increases: characters shrink below the vision encoder's effective resolution, making them indistinguishable."* The downscaling is silent — no error, no warning, and the token count looks fantastic right up to the point where the model is confidently reading blurred text.

## What the research says the real ceiling is

Three papers bracket the usable range. None of them claims you can compress indefinitely.

**DeepSeek-OCR** ([arXiv 2510.18234](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.18234), MIT-licensed, ~23.9k stars) framed the whole idea as "contexts optical compression" and put a number on the decay: when text tokens are within **10x** the vision tokens, OCR precision holds around **97%**. Push to **20x** compression and precision falls to roughly **60%**. Its encoder offers fixed modes — 512x512 at 64 vision tokens, 640x640 at 100, 1024x1024 at 256, 1280x1280 at 400 — and at 100 vision tokens per page it beat GOT-OCR2.0 running at 256 tokens per page on OmniDocBench, while under 800 tokens it beat MinerU2.0's 6,000-plus.

**Glyph** ([arXiv 2510.17800](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.17800), Tsinghua + Zhipu AI) attacked the context window rather than OCR: render long text to images, feed a VLM, and get **3–4x token compression** at accuracy comparable to Qwen3-8B on long-context benchmarks — plus roughly **4x faster prefilling and decoding** and about **2x faster SFT training**. Under aggressive compression, they report a 128K-context VLM reaching 1M-token-level text tasks. Notably, they did not hand-tune the rendering: an **LLM-driven genetic search** looked for the font, layout and resolution that balance accuracy against compression. Rendering configuration is a hyperparameter, not a detail.

**LensVLM** ([arXiv 2605.07019](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.07019), Apple, May 2026) added the missing piece — a way out of the cliff. Built on Qwen3.5-9B-Base, it ships at 5x, 10x and 15x compression levels and gives the model a `read_page` tool: **scan everything compressed, then expand only the pages you actually need** back to full resolution. It holds accuracy comparable to the full-text upper bound at **4.3x effective compression**, and beats retrieval-based, text-compression and visual-compression baselines out to **10.1x** across seven text-QA benchmarks. Weights are on Hugging Face as `apple/LensVLM-9B` under Apple's research model license; code is at [apple-aiml-research/ml-lensvlm](https://github.com/apple-aiml-research/ml-lensvlm).

## Why selective expansion beats retrieval (and when it doesn't)

On paper this looks like RAG with extra steps. It isn't, and the difference is worth understanding.

Retrieval **pre-indexes the entire corpus before any question exists**. You chunk, embed, and store — and every downstream answer inherits whatever your chunk boundaries destroyed: a table split across two chunks, a footnote severed from its claim, a function separated from its imports.

Optical scan-and-expand inverts that. The whole document enters the context **as compressed visual tokens, intact**, layout and all. The model reads the low-resolution version, decides which pages matter for *this* question, and pays full price only for those. No pre-indexing, no chunk boundaries, no embedding model to keep in sync with the corpus.

The LensVLM analysis adds a useful refinement: **text expansion works best for rendered text, while high-resolution image expansion suits native documents where layout carries task-relevant information.** Expanding a rendered page back to characters is cheaper and cleaner; expanding a scanned invoice to full resolution keeps the spatial structure you needed in the first place.

Where retrieval still wins: corpora far larger than any context window, anything that needs exact provenance and character offsets, and any pipeline where you must prove which source produced which sentence.

## Try it on your own documents in 15 minutes

Do not adopt this on the strength of anyone's aggregate ratio — including the ones on this page. The variables that decide it are yours: your font, your line length, your provider, your questions.

## When it is worth it — and when it is malpractice

**Reach for optical compression when:**

- The material is **long, static and read-only** — policy manuals, standards, API references, legal corpora you query repeatedly but never edit.
- The source is **already visual** — scanned PDFs, forms, invoices, slide decks. You were going to pay image tokens anyway; rendering is free.
- **Layout carries meaning** — tables, multi-column documents, anything where serialising to Markdown destroys the relationships you need.
- You need **many pages in view at once** and retrieval keeps slicing through the answer.

**Keep the text path when:**

- The model has to **edit, diff or patch** the content. A coding agent needs line numbers, exact whitespace and byte-accurate quoting; none of that survives a round trip through pixels.
- **A single wrong character is unacceptable** — account numbers, dosages, legal citations, config values. This is where the resolution cliff shows up first.
- The input is **short**. On Gemini you will pay more, and on any provider you are trading real accuracy for a rounding error in cost.
- You need **exact citation offsets** back into the source.
