On **September 28, 2026** AMD announced it is acquiring **World Labs** for about
**$8.2 billion in stock** — its second-largest deal ever, behind only Xilinx — and
Fei-Fei Li will join as executive vice president and chief scientist reporting to
Lisa Su. A day later OpenAI's DevDay took the front page. Between them, "world model"
became the phrase of the week.

Here is the problem with the phrase. Two products that share it are architecturally
opposite. One keeps the world **inside the model** and streams you frames as you move;
it is interactive, it cannot be exported, and a session is capped in seconds. The
other **generates a 3D scene** you download, own and render yourself for free; it is
persistent, engine-ready, and completely inert. Picking the wrong one is not a
preference mistake, it is a re-architecture.

This page is the practical split: what each family really produces, the verified API
and export details, the one benchmark that settles a common assumption, the open
weights you can run on your own GPU, and the five things neither family will do for
you.

## Two things share the name

|  | **Frame generators** | **3D generators** |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Genie 3 / Project Genie, driving world models | Marble (World Labs) |
| What it outputs | A video stream, frame by frame, conditioned on your actions | A scene: Gaussian splats, panoramas, optionally a mesh |
| Where the world lives | Inside the model's rolling context | In a file you download |
| Interactive? | **Yes** — that is the whole point | No. You navigate it, but it does not react |
| Exportable? | **No** geometry to export | Yes — SPZ, PLY, GLB, collider meshes |
| Cost shape | Per second of interaction, per viewer, forever | Once at generation. Rendering is then yours |
| Fails by | Drifting, forgetting, running out of session | Being static and not simulating anything |

If you need a world that **responds** — a player pressing a key, a robot policy taking
an action and seeing what happens — you need a frame generator. If you need a world
that **persists** — an asset in your game, a set in your render pipeline, a scene five
users load next month and see identically — you need a 3D generator. Almost every
confused "why can't it just…" question on this topic is someone holding one and asking
for the other.

## The frame generators, and the 60-second wall

Google's **Project Genie** went from research preview to a public-ish product on
**January 29, 2026**. The access shape is narrow and worth knowing before you plan
around it: **Google AI Ultra** subscribers, **United States only**, **18+**, and
explicitly **not** available on Google AI Ultra for Business. Google calls it an
"experimental research prototype".

The limit that teaches you the most is Google's own: **generations are limited to 60
seconds.** That is not a billing decision, it is a state problem. In a frame generator
the world is not stored anywhere — it is implied by the model's context. Every frame is
predicted from the prompt, the accumulated frames and your actions. Consistency is
therefore bounded by the same thing that bounds a long chat: how much history the model
can hold and keep coherent. Walk away from a wall and back, and whether the wall is
still there is a *memory* question, not a rendering one.

Google's stated caveats are the rest of the honest spec sheet: generated worlds "might
not look completely true-to-life or always adhere closely to prompts or images, or
real-world physics", and characters "can sometimes be less controllable, or experience
higher latency in control".

Read that physics line carefully if you were planning to use a frame generator as a
simulator. It is a plausibility engine, not a solver. Nothing guarantees momentum is
conserved between frame 300 and frame 301.

### The frame budget nobody mentions

Interactive generation at the commonly reported 24 fps gives you **about 41.7 ms per
frame**, end to end, including your input reaching the server. That single number
explains most of the product decisions in this family: why sessions are short, why
access is gated to a paid tier, why there is no public API yet, and why the cost curve
is *per viewer-second* rather than per asset. You are not buying a file. You are
renting a GPU that must beat a 42-millisecond deadline for as long as someone is
looking.

## The 3D generators, and what you actually receive

World Labs' **Marble** turns text, an image, a panorama, multiple views or a video clip
into a navigable 3D scene. The important engineering fact is the representation: the
output is **3D Gaussian splats**, not a mesh.

Splats are millions of small, oriented, semi-transparent blobs with colour and
view-dependent shading. They photo-reconstruct beautifully and render fast on
commodity GPUs. They also have no surfaces, no edges and no faces — which is why the
next section matters more than it looks.

What Marble's own export docs say you get:

| Export | Availability |
|---|---|
| Gaussian splats — **SPZ** and **PLY** | Standard plan |
| 360 panoramas | Standard plan |
| **Collider meshes** | Standard plan |
| Textured mesh (**GLB**) + commercial rights | **Pro plan only** |
| Anything at all | **Not on Free** — generate only |

Two gotchas live in that table. First, **collider meshes are a separate export** — you
need them because you cannot raycast, collide or do physics against splats directly.
The splats are what the player sees; the collider mesh is what the engine tests
against. Ship only the splats and your beautiful world has no floor. Second,
**commercial rights are tied to the Pro tier**, not to the act of exporting. Check the
plan before you put a generated world in a product.

Marble's docs also note that lower-resolution exports are "optimized to be as
perceptually similar as possible to the higher-resolution files" — a hint that you
should test the small tier before paying to ship the big one.

### SPZ: the 10× that is not World Labs'

A detail several write-ups get wrong: **SPZ is Niantic's format**, open-sourced by
Niantic Spatial, not a World Labs invention — Marble simply exports to it. It is worth
using anyway. SPZ stores each spherical-harmonics coefficient as an 8-bit signed
integer with optional further quantisation, landing at roughly **10× smaller than the
equivalent PLY** with no perceptible quality loss. Niantic's own testing puts around
**5 bits as the sweet spot** for most scenes, with essentially-zero MSE at 8 bits and
about 18× compression at 3. **SPZ 4**, released in 2026, splits compression into six
parallel ZSTD streams — one per attribute class — for roughly **3–5× faster
compression** and **1.5–2× faster end-to-end loading** at the same ~10× size
advantage.

If you are shipping splats over the web, that is the difference between a scene that
loads and one that does not.

## The API, for real

The **World API** exposes Marble's generation to your own code. The verified shape,
from World Labs' docs index and its official Python client:

- **Inputs**: text, images, panoramas, multi-view inputs, video.
- **Outputs**: splats in **SPZ, PLY, SOGS, KSPLAT** and **SPLAT**.
- **Auth**: an API key passed in the **`WLT-Api-Key`** header; the Python client reads
  `WORLDLABS_API_KEY` from the environment.
- **Execution model**: **asynchronous**. You submit a generation, get an operation
  back, and **poll until it is done**. There is also a credit-balance endpoint, media
  upload/retrieve, and world create/retrieve/list/export/delete.
- **Client**: `worldlabsai/worldlabs-api-python`, **Python 3.12+**. A CUDA GPU is only
  needed for local rendering, not for API use.
- **Pricing**: usage-based.

<PromptCard title="Generate a world from text and poll for it">
{`# github.com/worldlabsai/worldlabs-api-python  (Python 3.12+)
# export WORLDLABS_API_KEY=...

from worldlabs_api.client import WorldLabsClient
from worldlabs_api.models import WorldTextPrompt, WorldsGenerateRequest

client = WorldLabsClient(api_key="your-key-here")

request = WorldsGenerateRequest(
    display_name="Mystical Forest",
    world_prompt=WorldTextPrompt(text_prompt="A mystical forest"),
)

op = client.generate_world(request)      # returns an operation, not a world
done = client.poll_operation(op.operation_id)   # poll until done is true
world = done.response`}
</PromptCard>

Design your integration around that async boundary from day one. World generation is a
job, not a request — the same lesson as batch inference or video generation. If your
handler waits inline for a world, you will rewrite it.

### Rendering it without a game engine

World Labs also maintains **Spark**, an MIT-licensed 3D Gaussian splatting renderer for
**THREE.js / WebGL2**, so a generated world can ship as a web page. It loads `.PLY`,
`.SPZ`, `.SPLAT`, `.KSPLAT` and `.SOG`, fuses splats with ordinary meshes in the
THREE.js pipeline, and targets the ~98% of devices with WebGL2.

**Spark 2.0** (April 15, 2026) is the release that makes big scenes viable in a
browser: a level-of-detail **splat tree** that renders a fixed number of splats per
frame regardless of scene size, a streaming **`.RAD`** format that supports HTTP Range
requests so a coarse proxy appears almost instantly and refines progressively, and
**virtual splat paging** against a fixed GPU memory pool so scenes larger than GPU
memory still render — including on phones and Quest 3.

<PromptCard title="Put a generated world on the web">
{`npm install @sparkjsdev/spark three
# docs and live examples: https://sparkjs.dev
# repo: https://github.com/sparkjsdev/spark
#
# Export from Marble as .spz (≈10x smaller than .ply), serve it as a
# static file, and load it into a THREE.js scene with Spark.
# For scenes over a few hundred MB, convert to .RAD so the viewer can
# range-request a coarse proxy first.`}
</PromptCard>

Note what just happened to your cost curve. After generation, you are serving a static
file from a CDN and rendering on the user's GPU. **Serving the world is free.** That is
the inverse of the frame-generator model, where every viewer-second is inference.

## The evidence: WorldScore

"Just use a video model" is the most common shortcut here, and there is now a
benchmark that answers it. **WorldScore** (Stanford, `arXiv:2504.00983`) is a unified
benchmark for world generation across 3D, 4D, text-to-video and image-to-video models —
about **3,000 examples**, with metrics for controllability, consistency, perceptual
quality and motion, and explicit **3D consistency** and camera-control measures.

The result that should change a decision: on **WorldScore-Static**, dedicated 3D scene
generators lead — **WonderWorld at 72.69** and **LucidDreamer at 70.40** — clearly
above the video models. If your requirement is a geometrically consistent world you can
move a camera through, a video model is the wrong tool even when its individual frames
look better.

Two caveats. The leaderboard is live and moves as teams submit. And WorldScore-Static
measures exactly what its name says — a *static* world; strong static consistency tells
you nothing about dynamics.

## Open weights: Cosmos 3, and what AMD actually bought

You do not need a vendor's API to have a world model. NVIDIA published **Cosmos 3** —
an open **omnimodel** for physical AI — with weights on Hugging Face:

| Model | Size | Runs on |
|---|---|---|
| `nvidia/Cosmos3-Edge` | **4B** | Edge devices, robots, vision agents |
| `nvidia/Cosmos3-Nano` | **16B** = 8B reasoner + 8B generator | Workstation class, e.g. RTX PRO 6000 |
| `nvidia/Cosmos3-Super` | **64B** = 32B reasoner + 32B generator | Hopper / Blackwell, large-scale synthetic data |

Architecturally it is a **Mixture-of-Transformers** handling text, image, video, audio
and **action** in one model, pairing an autoregressive subsequence for reasoning with a
diffusion subsequence for generation. It is wired into Hugging Face Diffusers, so the
entry point is ordinary Python.

<PromptCard title="Run an open world model locally">
{`pip install --upgrade diffusers transformers accelerate

# Cosmos3-Nano is 16B (8B reasoner + 8B generator); check the model
# card on Hugging Face for the licence and the exact torch version
# before you build on it.

import torch
from diffusers import Cosmos3OmniPipeline

pipe = Cosmos3OmniPipeline.from_pretrained(
    "nvidia/Cosmos3-Nano", torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16
)`}
</PromptCard>

Now the part that explains the price tag. Cosmos 3's task table is not a list of video
tricks:

| Input | Output | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| Text / image / video | Video | Video generation |
| Text / video | Text | Vision-language reasoning |
| **Action** + image + text | Video | **Forward dynamics** — "if I do this, what happens?" |
| Text + **video** | **Action** | **Inverse dynamics** — "what actions produced this?" |
| Image + text | Video **and action** | **Policy generation** |

Inverse dynamics is the quiet one. It turns footage into **labelled actions** — which
means ordinary video becomes robot training data, and a generated world becomes a place
to train and evaluate a policy without hardware. AMD's own release describes World Labs
as building models that "generate, reconstruct and simulate interactive 3D
environments" *plus* technology for "robotic learning and simulation", and frames the
deal around reasoning, robotics, simulation and physical AI informing its future
silicon roadmap.

Read as a compute story rather than a graphics story, $8.2 billion for a
training-environment flywheel is a different kind of bet than $8.2 billion for a
text-to-3D tool.

## Five jobs a world model will not do for you

## Choosing, in one pass

- **Game or product asset, multiple users, must look the same tomorrow** → 3D
  generator. Export SPZ for the web or GLB for an engine, and remember the collider.
- **Interactive exploration, one user, novelty is the point** → frame generator, and
  design inside a 60-second session.
- **Training or evaluating a policy** → open weights with forward and inverse
  dynamics, on your own GPU, with a real simulator for anything safety-relevant.
- **A camera moving through a consistent scene** → not a video model. WorldScore says
  so.
