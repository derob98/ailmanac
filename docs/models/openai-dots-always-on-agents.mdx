<LevelBadge level="intermediate" />

On **September 29, 2026**, at DevDay, OpenAI launched **dots**: named, persistent
agents that live inside ChatGPT, each with **its own cloud computer and browser**,
each running on **GPT-6 Astra**, each continuing to work while you are logged off.
You reach the same dot from the ChatGPT app, from Slack, from Microsoft Teams, or
by calling it on the phone, and it remembers the project either way.

Every write-up of the launch describes the demo. Almost none describe the part that
decides whether you can actually let one of these loose on your accounts: the
**permission architecture**. That is what this page is about — plus an honest
side-by-side with what Anthropic already ships, because the two companies have
solved the same problem at deliberately different layers.

## The one-paragraph version

A dot is a **named, persistent agent** rather than a conversation. It runs on GPT-6
Astra, gets a **dedicated cloud computer and browser** whose sessions are separate
from your own logins, reaches **4,000+ apps** through OpenAI's plugin ecosystem, and
keeps working when you close the tab. When you are not talking to it, it does
**proactive research** — scanning your connected apps with a **read-only** subset of
its tools to work out what to do next. Everything it does passes an **automatic
review** against your instructions, your Custom Rules and OpenAI's built-in safety
requirements before it executes. Your first dot is included in eligible plans; more
are promised as a paid add-on.

## What is genuinely new here

Hosted agent loops are not new — OpenAI shipped the [Agents API in
September](/docs/models/openai-agents-api-vs-managed-agents), Anthropic has run
[Managed Agents](/docs/api/managed-agents) since April. Three things about dots are
new, and they are product decisions, not model decisions.

**1. The agent is the unit, not the session.** Managed Agents and the Agents API both
give you durable *sessions* that a developer creates and destroys. A dot is a thing
you name, that persists across surfaces, and that a non-developer owns. The mental
model is closer to "a colleague's Slack handle" than "an API resource."

**2. Idle time is productive by design.** Most agent runtimes are request-driven: you
call, it works, it stops. A dot's default state is *looking for something to do*.
That inverts the failure mode. The question stops being "did my agent finish the
task" and becomes "what did my agent decide to look at while I was asleep."

**3. Identity is split from your identity.** The dot browses on its own machine with
its own sessions. That is the opposite of the pattern in
[shared-login browsers for agents](/docs/models/browser-agents-shared-login), where
the whole point is that the agent inherits *your* cookies. Separation is safer and
also weaker: some sites block cloud browsers or demand extra verification, and a dot
that needs your real session has to ask.

## The permission ladder

This is the part worth reading twice. OpenAI's constraint design is five layers, and
they are not all the same *kind* of control — some are architectural, some are
policy, and only one of them is yours to configure freely.

  },

  },
  {

  },
  {
    title: "Automatic review and monitoring",
    body: "Before an action runs, an automatic review checks it against your instructions, your Custom Rules and the safety requirements. Monitoring can pause or stop the dot's work mid-run. This is the rung that catches the case the other four do not: an action that is individually permitted but wrong in context."
  }

Above all five sits one hard boundary: **certain sensitive tasks, such as changing a
password, always stay with you.** Not as a default you can flip — as a structural
exclusion.

### Why rung 3 is the interesting one

Most "safe agent" marketing describes rules the model is *told* to follow. Rung 3 is a
different category: during proactive research the write tools are **not in the
loop at all**. An agent cannot misuse a tool it was not given.

This is the same principle AILmanac's security section keeps arriving at from the
attack side — see
[prompt injection](/docs/security/prompt-injection) and
[hardening autonomous runs](/docs/security/hardening-autonomous-runs). Instructions
in a model's context are *data*, and data can be overwritten by other data. Tool
bindings are *configuration*, and a compromised prompt cannot rebind them.

The practical consequence for you: **the dangerous moment is not proactive research,
it is the handoff.** Reading is read-only. The instant a dot proposes an action and
you approve it, you are back to a normal agent with normal write tools, acting on
conclusions it reached while reading content you did not review. Treat the approval
prompt as the trust boundary.

## Reading the safety numbers honestly

Three figures from the GPT-6 Astra system card get quoted in dots coverage. Each
measures something narrower than it sounds.

| Figure | What it says | What it does not say |
|---|---|---|
| **0 scored attack successes** in 100 rollouts across 50,000 emails | Bulk malicious-email generation was not achievable in that test harness | Nothing about targeted, low-volume misuse, or about injected content in mail the dot *reads* |
| **91.8% alignment pass rate** on permission changes | In roughly 9 of 10 trials the model handled a permission-change scenario as intended | The remaining ~8% is a permission decision going the wrong way. On an always-on agent, that fraction is sampled continuously, not once |
| **19.7% moderate flags** with ten chained tasks, no severe breach or exfiltration observed | Long chains raise concern flags about one run in five | "Moderate" is a grading label from the vendor's own rubric, and "no severe breach observed" is an observation, not a proof |

The useful reading is not "is 91.8% good." It is **rate times exposure**. A one-shot
assistant you invoke twenty times a day samples that distribution twenty times. An
always-on agent with proactive research samples it whenever it decides to wake up,
which is a number OpenAI has not published.

## Dots vs what Anthropic already ships

There is no Claude product that maps one-to-one, because Anthropic put the same
capability one layer lower — in the API — and left the packaging to you.

| | **OpenAI dots** | **Claude Managed Agents** | **Claude Cowork / agent teams** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who creates one | Any user on an eligible plan, in the ChatGPT desktop app | A developer, via API | A user, in the desktop workspace |
| Persistence unit | The dot (named, long-lived) | Agent + session + environment | The workspace and its threads |
| Runs when you are away | Yes, by design — proactive research | Yes, via **scheduled deployments (cron)** | Tied to the workspace |
| Who decides when to act | The model | Your schedule or your call | You |
| Own compute + browser | Yes, per dot | Yes, environments/sandboxes | Local desktop context |
| Hard cost cap | Not published per dot; absorbed into plan allowances | **Yes** — [session budgets](/docs/api/managed-agents-session-budgets) with a `budget_reached` stop reason | Plan-based |
| Reachable from Slack / Teams / phone | Yes | Whatever you build | Desktop |

Two differences do real work:

**Model-initiated vs schedule-initiated.** Anthropic's unattended path is a cron
deployment: *you* choose when it runs. A dot chooses. Model-initiated wake-ups are
strictly more useful and strictly harder to reason about, because your audit question
changes from "what ran at 03:00" to "why did it think 03:00 was the moment."

**A dollar cap is a primitive on one side and not on the other.** Managed Agents will
stop a session at a hard list-rate ceiling and tell you why. A dot's cost is folded
into plan allowances — which is friendlier until you want a per-agent budget for an
unattended process. If that is your requirement, it is an argument for building on an
API rather than adopting a product.

If you want the developer-layer comparison in full, that is a separate page:
[OpenAI Agents API vs Claude Managed Agents](/docs/models/openai-agents-api-vs-managed-agents).

## Plans, and the cut that shipped the same day

Dots are gated to paid tiers — reporting at launch points to **ChatGPT Pro**,
**Business Premium** and **Enterprise**, with Enterprise, Edu and Healthcare
workspaces **off by default** until an admin enables them. Your first dot is included;
additional dots and higher throughput are promised as a paid add-on with terms not yet
announced. Chatting with a dot does not count toward your ChatGPT usage limits, but
the work it runs in **Codex or ChatGPT Work does**.

Landing the same day, and much less discussed:

- **Pro 200's included usage halves on October 30** — from roughly 20× the Plus
  allowance to 10×, across ChatGPT Work and Codex, with GPT-6 Pro messages dropping
  from 200 to 100 per week. Existing subscribers keep current limits through October 29
  and get a one-time **$2,500 usage credit** expiring at the end of the year.
- **A new $500/month Pro 500 tier** — reported at 25× the Plus allowance, and the only
  Pro plan with **Ultrafast**, which OpenAI describes as up to **8× faster token
  generation in Codex** (around 300 tokens/second).

Read those two together. The headline is a more capable agent; the small print is that
the allowance feeding it got smaller at the tier most power users are on. That is the
number to model before you plan a workflow around a dot.

## The limits nobody has published

Worth holding in mind before you plan anything load-bearing around a dot:

- **Concurrency** — how many tasks one dot runs at once: unpublished.
- **Storage and hardware** on the cloud computer: unpublished.
- **Memory horizon** — how long a dot's notes and context survive across weeks or
  months: unpublished. OpenAI describes the dot as carrying relevant ChatGPT memory
  plus its own notes, and states those notes are **not a complete transcript**. Plan
  for lossy recall, not a log.
- **Wake-up frequency** for proactive research: unpublished, and it is the variable
  that drives both usefulness and cost.
- **Site compatibility** — some sites block cloud browsers or require additional
  verification, so a dot can be fully permitted and still unable to complete a task.

On training data: OpenAI states it does not train on proactive research or on a dot's
notes to itself, and that Business, Enterprise and Edu workspace content is not used
to improve models by default; on personal plans you control whether a dot's
conversations and work are used.
