<LevelBadge level="advanced" />

Every model on AILmanac so far generates text the same way: one token, then the
next, left to right, forever. A different family of models does not. It starts
with a blank canvas of masked positions, predicts **all of them at once**, keeps
the ones it is confident about, and repeats. Position 40 can be decided before
position 7.

The numbers are the reason anyone cares. Inception's Mercury 2 — a *reasoning*
model — reportedly runs at **1,009 tokens/sec on Blackwell GPUs with 1.7 s
end-to-end latency**, roughly 8–14× faster than comparable reasoning-enabled
competitors ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/inception-launches-mercury-2-the-first-diffusion-based-language-reasoning-model/)).
Google's experimental Gemini Diffusion samples at **1,479 tokens/sec excluding
overhead**, against Gemini 2.5 Flash's 272.4
([Techmeme roundup](https://www.techmeme.com/250521/p54)).

And yet the README of LLaDA — the 8B open-weight model that proved the approach
at scale — tells you its quality peaks when the number of sampling steps **equals
the response length**. One forward pass per token. Exactly like an
autoregressive model, but without a KV cache, so *slower*.

Both facts are true. This lesson is the bridge between them: what the algorithm
actually does, which knob buys the speed and what it costs, the two structural
limits that made early diffusion LLMs unusable and the fix that shipped, and the
three gotchas (streaming, length billing, and a jailbreak class that does not
exist on autoregressive models) you need before you put one in production.

## The mechanism, in one diagram you have to imagine

An autoregressive (AR) model's loop is: read the whole prefix, emit a probability
distribution over **one** position (the next one), sample, append, repeat. The
KV cache is what makes this cheap — the keys and values for every earlier token
were computed once and never change, because attention is causal and nothing to
the right exists yet.

A masked diffusion LLM inverts that. Attention is **bidirectional**: every
position attends to every other position, in both directions. So in a single
forward pass the model produces a distribution for *every* masked position
simultaneously. Generation becomes an iterative refinement of a whole answer,
not a walk along it.

That last line is the one worth memorising. An AR model is pinned at one token
per forward pass. A diffusion LLM lets you choose. DiffusionGemma, Google's
open-weight entry, reports roughly **20 tokens per forward pass** while refining
blocks of 256 in parallel, which is how it reaches ~1,500 output tokens/sec on a
single H100 ([DiffusionGemma Technical Report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00146)).

## The steps knob, and why cranking it is wrong rather than just lossy

Dream 7B exposes the knob explicitly. Its `diffusion_generate()` documents
`steps` as "diffusion timesteps" where `max_new_tokens / steps` tokens are
produced per step, and notes plainly that fewer steps are faster but coarser
([DreamLM/Dream](https://github.com/DreamLM/Dream), Apache-2.0).

So why not always use few steps? Because the error it introduces is not noise —
it is a specific, nameable bug.

A single forward pass gives you, for each masked slot, a **marginal**
distribution: the probability of a token *at that position*, averaged over
everything still unknown. If you commit two slots from the same pass, you are
sampling from the **product of their marginals**, not from their joint
distribution. Any dependency between them is silently discarded. The Fast-dLLM
authors state the root cause directly: generation quality degrades under parallel
decoding because masked diffusion models sample tokens independently from
marginal probabilities when the true joint distribution has dependencies
([Fast-dLLM, arXiv:2505.22618](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.22618)).

The intuition: ask for a city and two adjacent slots might score "New" and
"Delhi" as the most likely token *for their own position*, because "New York"
and "New Delhi" are both plausible. Commit both in one pass and you get neither.
The failure mode of over-parallel decoding is not vaguer prose, it is confidently
assembled nonsense at the seams.

The fix that made the field practical is **confidence-aware parallel decoding**:
compute a confidence per masked slot (typically the max softmax probability),
commit only the slots above a threshold, and if none clear it, commit the single
highest so the loop still makes progress. Tokens that are nearly independent get
decoded together; tokens that depend on each other wait. Fast-dLLM reports up to
**27.6× throughput improvement** on LLaDA and Dream across benchmarks with
minimal accuracy loss — training-free, just a decoding change.

That is the shape of this entire research area: the parallelism is free, the
*independence* is not, and the work is in detecting which tokens can safely go
together.

<Callout type="note" items={["A confidence threshold is a quality dial you can actually reason about: higher threshold means fewer tokens committed per pass, more passes, slower, closer to AR quality. Fast-dLLM v2 reports a 2.6× speedup on GSM8K at threshold 0.9 with minimal accuracy loss — that is the kind of setting to start from, not 'commit everything'."]} />

## The two structural limits — and block diffusion, which is why you can call one today

LLaDA's own repository lists its efficiency constraints without euphemism: fixed
context length for sampling, **no KV-cache support**, and degraded performance
when sampling steps drop below the response length. The authors frame it as
deliberate — they wanted to find the ceiling of the approach before optimising it
([ML-GSAI/LLaDA](https://github.com/ML-GSAI/LLaDA), MIT, ~4k stars, Base and
Instruct weights plus LLaDA 1.5, LLaDA-MoE-7B-A1B and iLLaDA variants).

Stack those up and a pure masked dLLM is nearly unusable as a product:

1. **No KV cache.** Bidirectional attention means every pass can change any
   token's context, so cached keys and values are invalid. You recompute the
   whole sequence every step.
2. **Fixed output length.** You must pick L before generating. Too small truncates;
   too large wastes compute on positions you will throw away.
3. **Latency worse than AR.** Points 1 and 2 together can make a "parallel" model
   slower end-to-end than the sequential one it replaces.

**Block diffusion** resolves all three by refusing to be pure. Generate in
blocks: condition on previous blocks **autoregressively**, run diffusion **inside**
the current block. That gives flexible sequence length and KV caching between
blocks — the BD3-LM formulation, an ICLR 2025 oral
([arXiv:2503.09573](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.09573),
[kuleshov-group/bd3lms](https://github.com/kuleshov-group/bd3lms)).

Fast-dLLM v2 ([arXiv:2509.26328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.26328)) shows what a
tuned version looks like, and the details are worth knowing because they explain
the vendor behaviour you will observe:

- **Hybrid attention mask.** Block-diagonal (bidirectional) *within* a block,
  block-causal (left-to-right) *between* blocks. Bidirectional context where it
  helps, AR semantics where the cache lives.
- **Two cache levels.** A block-level cache holding finished blocks as clean
  prefix context, plus a sub-block cache (DualCache) for the recomputation that
  intra-block decoding still needs.
- **Complementary masking in training.** Each sample is duplicated into two views
  with masks `m` and `1 − m`, so every token is supervised both as a mask target
  and as context.
- **Reported results.** Up to 2.5× faster than standard AR decoding; 1.8× at
  batch size 64 on H100s; a 7B average benchmark score of 60.3 against Dream's
  57.6, including HumanEval 63.4 vs 57.9 and GSM8K 62.0 vs 39.2.

And the training cost is the detail that reframes the field: Fast-dLLM v2 reports
fine-tuning from an AR model on roughly **1B tokens — about 500× less than
Dream's 580B** — a 7B trained in ~12 hours on 64 A100s.

## Diffusion is a decoding mode you can retrofit, not a separate species

DiffusionGemma makes that point unmissable. It is not trained from scratch: it
fine-tunes **Gemma 4**, a mixture-of-experts model with 3.8B activated and 25.2B
total parameters, into a diffusion model using **fewer than 10% of the starting
AR model's total training token budget** — supervised denoising fine-tuning,
then reinforcement learning and sampler distillation
([arXiv:2608.00146](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00146), CC-BY-4.0 weights).

What survives the conversion is the surprising part. The report states it retains
the base model's thinking mode, multimodal inputs and long contexts — and
**keeps the ability to generate autoregressively** with only minor degradation.
One set of weights, two decoding modes.

If that result holds up, "diffusion LLM" stops being a distinct product category
and becomes a serving option: the same family, with a parallelism dial on the
inference side. That is a far more consequential claim than any single
tokens-per-second record, and it is the reason to learn the mechanism now rather
than when a provider flips a flag on a model you already use.

## What a diffusion LLM is actually better at

Speed is the headline and the weakest part of the case, because speed is
contested — specialised AR hardware is also delivering hundreds of tokens per
second (see [Token Speed](/docs/models/fast-inference-and-token-speed)). The
durable advantages are the ones that follow from the *shape* of the algorithm.

**Any-order generation and native infilling.** Because you choose which slot to
commit, you can leave a hole in the middle of a sequence and have the model fill
it with full context on both sides. Dream exposes this as the `alg` parameter —
`origin` (random order), `maskgit_plus` (top-1 confidence), `topk_margin`
(top1 − top2 margin), `entropy` — plus `alg_temp` to add randomness to those
strategies. For code edits, template completion, or filling fields in a fixed
JSON skeleton, that is a structurally better fit than asking an AR model to
re-emit a whole file to change line 40.

**Latency-bound interaction.** Inception's Mercury Voice advertises
time-to-first-token **under 170 ms** on a 128K context, priced at $0.20/$1.50 per
million input/output tokens at the time of writing. Voice is the clearest case
where latency *is* the product; our [full-duplex voice AI](/docs/models/full-duplex-voice-ai)
page covers why that threshold matters.

**End-to-end reasoning turnaround.** Mercury 2's reported 1.7 s for a complete
reasoning answer is a different claim from throughput — it is the number an agent
loop actually feels, turn after turn.

**Bounded answers instead of prose.** Inception's Mercury Decide returns a
choice, a score, or a yes/no with a calibrated probability read off the model
rather than written out as text — reported at up to 14 decisions per second,
which makes "run it on every case and escalate the unsure ones" affordable. The
category and its caveats are covered in depth on
[System One decision models](/docs/models/system-one-decision-models-jev-kev);
the diffusion backbone is an implementation detail there, not the point.

**Price pressure.** Mercury 2.5 currently lists a 260K context at $0.04/$0.15 per
million input/output tokens (promotional, off $0.20/$0.75) with cached input at
$0.004 per million. Treat promo pricing as temporary and model your bill at list.

## Three gotchas before you ship one

<Callout type="warning" items={["Tokens/sec is measured at the vendor's chosen parallelism. Your quality bar may require more steps, which costs you the speedup. Benchmark on your own prompts at your own accuracy threshold.", "Output is not final left-to-right. Only block boundaries are monotonic; inside a block, slots can still be masked or revised. Any UI or parser that assumes 'token N will never change' is wrong.", "A pure masked dLLM bills and computes a length you chose in advance. Block diffusion reduces that to per-block granularity — it does not remove it."]} />

The third one deserves more than a bullet, because it is a security property and
it has no autoregressive analogue.

An AR model's alignment has a structural advantage: the refusal happens *first*.
If the model decides to decline, every subsequent token is conditioned on
"I can't help with that." A diffusion LLM has no such moment. There is no
left-to-right point at which a refusal takes over the rest of the generation —
masked slots get filled to be consistent with whatever is already on the canvas.

DIJA ("The Devil behind the mask", ICLR 2026,
[arXiv:2507.11097](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.11097),
[ZichenWen1/DIJA](https://github.com/ZichenWen1/DIJA)) turns that into an attack.
The adversarial prompt puts the harmful intent in **unmasked** tokens and leaves
masked spans for the model to complete in a contextually consistent way. The
reported results: up to **100% keyword-based attack success rate on
Dream-Instruct**, surpassing the strongest prior baseline by up to 78.5% in
evaluator-based ASR on JailbreakBench and by 37.7 points in StrongREJECT score —
while requiring **no rewriting or obfuscation of the harmful content**. The
authors' conclusion is that existing alignment mechanisms do not protect dLLMs
against context-aware masked-input prompts, because the model cannot filter an
unsafe generation mid-inference the way a sequential one can.

A follow-up ([arXiv:2602.00388](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.00388)) argues the
picture is two-sided — diffusion confers a safety *blessing* in some settings
that then breaks under context — which is a reason to measure rather than to
assume either way.

Practical takeaway: if you expose an open-weight dLLM, do not treat its
instruction tuning as a guardrail. Filter and classify at the boundary, on both
the request and the response, exactly as you would for a base model. Our
[securing local and hybrid agents](/docs/models/securing-local-and-hybrid-agents)
page covers the boundary pattern.

<PromptCard title="The steps sweep on Dream 7B — find your own quality cliff">{`import time, torch
from transformers import AutoModel, AutoTokenizer

NAME = "Dream-org/Dream-v0-Instruct-7B"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(NAME, trust_remote_code=True)
model = AutoModel.from_pretrained(
    NAME, torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16, trust_remote_code=True
).to("cuda").eval()

PROMPT = "List the five largest cities in Germany with their populations."
MAX_NEW = 256

# steps == MAX_NEW is the authors' best-quality setting: one token per
# forward pass. Each halving doubles tokens-per-pass and doubles the
# number of independence assumptions you are making.
for steps in [256, 128, 64, 32, 16]:
    inputs = tok.apply_chat_template(
        [{"role": "user", "content": PROMPT}],
        return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True, add_generation_prompt=True,
    ).to("cuda")

    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    out = model.diffusion_generate(
        inputs.input_ids,
        attention_mask=inputs.attention_mask,
        max_new_tokens=MAX_NEW,
        steps=steps,
        temperature=0.0,
        alg="entropy",          # commit the lowest-entropy slots first
        alg_temp=0.0,
    )
    dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
    text = tok.decode(out[0][len(inputs.input_ids[0]):], skip_special_tokens=True)

    print(f"--- steps={steps}  tokens/pass={MAX_NEW/steps:.0f}  {dt:.2f}s ---")
    print(text.strip()[:400])

# Read the OUTPUTS, not just the timings. The numbers and the city names are
# the multi-token units that break first when too many slots are committed
# from one pass. That corruption is the conditional-independence bug, visible.`}</PromptCard>
