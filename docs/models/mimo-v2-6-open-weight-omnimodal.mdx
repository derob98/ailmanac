<LevelBadge level="intermediate" />

On **September 22, 2026**, Xiaomi published **MiMo-V2.6** — three checkpoints on
Hugging Face under a plain **MIT license**, headlined by a **1.02-trillion-parameter**
sparse Mixture-of-Experts model with a **1-million-token context** that takes
text, image, video and audio as input. It landed the same day as Claude Opus 5.5
and GPT-6 Sol/Luna, and it went straight to the top of Hacker News.

The headline write-ups all say roughly the same thing: frontier-class open
weights, twenty to sixty times cheaper than closed models. That part is true and
easy to check. What nobody has written up is the part that decides whether you
should actually use it — which benchmark versions the wins came from, why the
flagship is not a machine you own, and what the third price tier is for.

## The one-sentence version

MiMo-V2.6-Pro is an **MIT-licensed, 1.02T-total / 42B-active** sparse MoE with a
**1M-token context** and **text, image, video and audio input** that ties or beats
Claude Opus 5 on short-horizon agentic and automation benchmarks, trails it badly
on long-horizon terminal work, and costs roughly one-tenth as much per token when
you rent it instead of running it.

## Four things that aren't obvious from the headlines

### 1. The Terminal-Bench win and the Terminal-Bench loss are the same model

This is the one to internalise. Launch coverage widely reported MiMo-V2.6-Pro at
**89.9 on Terminal Bench 2.1**, just ahead of Claude Opus 5's 89.1. Xiaomi's own
model card does not report Terminal Bench 2.1 at all. It reports **Terminal Bench
4.0**, where MiMo-V2.6-Pro scores **34.9** against Opus 5's **49.0** — a 14.1-point
deficit.

Both numbers are real. Terminal Bench 2.1 is an older, largely saturated harness
where every frontier model clusters near 90 and a 0.8-point gap is noise.
Terminal Bench 4.0 is the current one, and it is where models are still spread
out. If you read "beats Claude on Terminal Bench" anywhere, the first question
is always *which version* — because on agentic suites the version change is
usually a bigger effect than the model change.

The same pattern shows up in AILmanac's coverage of
[DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash](/docs/models/deepseek-v4-1-flash-open-weight-1m) and
[Gemini 3.8 Flash](/docs/models/gemini-3-8-flash): parity on DeepSWE, a 15–20
point hole on Terminal-Bench 4.0. It is becoming the signature shape of the cheap
tier.

### 2. A 33-point generational jump is a harness story, not an intelligence story

The model card's own table includes the previous generation. MiMo-V2.5-Pro scored
**19.0** on DeepSWE v1.1 and **1.5** on Terminal Bench 4.0. V2.6-Pro scores 71.9
and 34.9.

A model does not get 23× better at terminal work in one generation. A score of
1.5 means the old model essentially could not complete the benchmark's tool-call
protocol — it was failing at the harness, not at the reasoning. Read enormous
generational deltas on *agentic* benchmarks as "we fixed tool calling" first, and
only then as capability. The deltas on non-agentic benchmarks are the honest ones:
ProgramBench went 12.5 → 26.5, roughly a doubling, which is a large but believable
generational step.

### 3. The 1M context is bought with attention layout, not brute force

MiMo-V2.6-Pro's LLM backbone is **70 layers: 60 sliding-window attention, 10 global
attention** — a 6:1 ratio. Sliding-window layers only keep a local KV window, so
the per-token KV footprint at 1M tokens is a fraction of what a fully global
70-layer stack would need. The ten global layers are what still let information
travel end to end.

The MoE is **384 routed experts with 8 active per token**, giving 42B active out of
1.02T — about **4.1% of the model runs on any given token**. Flash uses the same
shape at 309–311B total and ~15B active. This is why a trillion-parameter model
can be served at all: you pay 1T in memory and 42B in compute.

On top of that sits a **5-layer multi-token-prediction speculative decoder that
drafts 7 tokens per pass**. That is a throughput mechanism, and it matters for the
next point.

### 4. "Omnimodal" means omnimodal *in*, and UltraSpeed sells latency, not quality

Two things the marketing language blurs:

**Modalities are input-side.** The card lists text, image, video and audio as
modalities, with a 681M-parameter MiMo ViT vision encoder and a 308M
AudioTokenizer plus a 127M audio patch encoder. The Hugging Face pipeline tag for
Pro is text generation. You can feed it a video and ask questions; it answers in
text. It is not a speech or image *generator*. If you want generation across
media, that is a different page:
[generative media](/docs/models/generative-media-image-audio-video).

**UltraSpeed is the same model at 10× the price.** `MiMo-V2.6-Pro-UltraSpeed` is
described as Pro quality at up to 20× the output speed, listed at roughly ten
times Pro's per-token price. There is no separate set of weights on Hugging Face
for it — it is a serving tier built on the speculative decoder and Xiaomi's
infrastructure. That is a perfectly reasonable product, but price it as what it
is: you are buying tokens-per-second for interactive and real-time workloads, not
accuracy. If your job is a background batch, UltraSpeed is a 10× overpay.

## The benchmark table, as the vendor published it

All figures below are **Xiaomi's own runs**, from the MiMo-V2.6-Pro-RL model card.
No independent replication existed at the time of writing.

| Benchmark | MiMo-V2.6 Pro | MiMo-V2.6 Flash | MiMo-V2.5 Pro | Claude Opus 5 | GPT-5.6 Sol | Claude Fable 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSWE v1.1 | 71.9 | 67.9 | 19.0 | **74.0** | 73.0 | 70.0 |
| ProgramBench | 26.5 | 26.0 | 12.5 | **37.0** | 25.0 | 33.0 |
| MiMo Code Bench | 63.2 | 61.2 | 40.4 | **68.6** | 59.3 | — |
| AutomationBench v1.0.6 | **53.1** | 52.3 | 16.0 | 50.3 | 45.8 | 46.2 |
| Toolathlon-Verified | 76.9 | 73.6 | 49.1 | **80.6** | 74.9 | 77.9 |
| Terminal Bench 4.0 | 34.9 | 28.8 | 1.5 | **49.0** | 39.9 | 42.4 |
| OSWorld-Verified | 82.0 | 80.8 | — | 83.4 | 83.0 | **86.0** |
| CyberGym | 94.0 | **95.1** | 40.0 | — | — | — |
| MiMo VisualCoding | 72.3 | 71.5 | — | 70.0 | **73.4** | 69.1 |

Three readings worth having:

- **Flash is within a few points of Pro almost everywhere.** 67.9 vs 71.9 on
  DeepSWE, 52.3 vs 53.1 on AutomationBench, and it *beats* Pro on CyberGym. At
  roughly a third of Pro's hosted price and a third of the parameters, Flash is
  the default unless you have measured a reason otherwise.
- **The competitor columns for CyberGym are empty.** Xiaomi reported 94.0 and 95.1
  and published no Claude or GPT comparison. Treat an unopposed number as
  unopposed — it tells you the model is competent at the task, not that it leads.
- **Pro wins exactly one row outright: AutomationBench.** Everything else is a
  near-tie or a loss. "Frontier-class open weights" is fair; "beats Claude" is not.

On the aggregate side, Artificial Analysis' Intelligence Index v4.3 placed
MiMo-V2.6-Pro at roughly **46**, reported as first among open-weight models and
level with Grok 4.7, against about 53 for the closed frontier. A seven-point
index gap for a tenth of the price is the actual pitch.

## What it costs, rented and owned

**Rented**, as reported at launch from the OpenRouter and Xiaomi listings, per
million tokens:

| Model | Input | Output |
|---|---|---|
| MiMo-V2.6-Flash | $0.14 | $0.28 |
| MiMo-V2.6-Pro | $0.435 | $0.87 |
| MiMo-V2.6-Pro-UltraSpeed | $4.35 | $8.70 |

For scale, Claude Opus 5.5 lists at $4 / $20 (see the
[Opus 5.5 field guide](/docs/models/opus-5-5-field-guide)). Pro is roughly **9×
cheaper on input and 23× cheaper on output**. UltraSpeed costs about the same as
Opus 5.5 on input and less than half on output — which is the honest way to think
about that tier.

**Owned** is a different conversation, and the launch commands give it away. The
model card's SGLang example for Pro asks for `--tp 16 --dp 2 ... --nnodes 2`:
tensor parallelism across sixteen devices, two data-parallel replicas, two nodes.
That is a rack, not a workstation. The vLLM path is lighter at
`--tensor-parallel-size 8`, still eight accelerators for a 1.02T checkpoint in
BF16.

Flash, at ~309–311B total and ~15B active, is the variant that a serious
single-node box can plausibly serve. The **MiMo-V2.6-Distill-Qwen-9B** checkpoint
— a 9B distillation, listed on Hugging Face under image-text-to-text — is the one
that fits on a laptop-class GPU. If your goal is "run a capable open model at
home", start there and at
[Run models locally with Ollama](/docs/models/run-models-locally-ollama), not at
Pro.

One more ownership caveat: the repositories published are the **post-RL
checkpoints** (`-Pro-RL`, `-Flash-RL`). No base checkpoint was released. You can
serve, quantise and adapt these, but you cannot start your own post-training from
the pre-RL base.

## When to reach for it, and when not

**Good fit**

- High-volume classification, extraction or automation over mixed media, where
  $0.14/M input changes what is economically possible at all.
- Anything you must run on your own hardware for privacy or jurisdiction reasons
  and that needs vision or audio understanding — few MIT-licensed models offer
  that combination. See
  [a private local AI stack](/docs/models/private-local-ai-stack).
- Short, bounded agentic tasks with well-specified tools, which is where
  AutomationBench and Toolathlon scores say it is genuinely competitive.

**Poor fit**

- Long autonomous coding sessions. The Terminal Bench 4.0 gap is 14 points against
  Opus 5 and 5 points against GPT-5.6 Sol, and it is the vendor's own number.
- Anywhere you need a base checkpoint to post-train from. Only RL checkpoints
  shipped.
- Single-box self-hosting of Pro. Budget for Flash or the 9B distill, or rent Pro.

## What Xiaomi disclosed that most labs do not

Worth noting because it is unusually specific: Xiaomi published the RL training
run's shape and cost — roughly a six-day run, **30 RL steps**, about **750,000
trajectories**, **1,568 prompts with 16 rollouts per step**, at a reported
**~$2.62M for Pro and ~$850K for Flash**, using asynchronous GRPO with the MoE
router frozen during RL.

Two things to take from it. First, a frozen router during reinforcement learning
is a stability choice: it keeps expert assignment fixed so the policy update
cannot quietly reshape which experts fire. Second, and more useful: **a
frontier-adjacent post-training run now costs single-digit millions**, not
hundreds. That is the number that explains why four labs shipped in the same week.
