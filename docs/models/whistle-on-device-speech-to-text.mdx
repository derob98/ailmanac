On 8 October 2026 a blog post titled *"Whistle: Speech to Text in 16.9 MB"*
reached 768 points on Hacker News. The number in the title is the whole pitch,
and it is true: the entire multilingual speech recognition model is a single
16.9 MB file that runs on a CPU with no GPU and no dependencies.

The discussion that followed split cleanly in two. People who read the
benchmark chart were impressed. People who pointed a microphone at it were
not — one tester reported 70 correct out of 170 utterances. Both groups were
right, and the reason they were both right is printed in the vendor's own
chart. It is just in the bars that carry no number.

## The one-sentence version

Whistle buys its size by quantizing a small encoder–decoder ASR model to 2–4
bits and band-limiting its front end to 250–3500 Hz, which costs it almost
nothing on close-microphone single-speaker speech — where it genuinely beats
Whisper base — and costs it a great deal on far-field, overlapping or accented
speech, where it is the worst of the three models in its own comparison.

## What is actually in the 16.9 MB

| Component | Detail |
|---|---|
| Front end | 25 ms window, 10 ms hop, 80 log-mel bins, band-limited 250–3500 Hz |
| Stem | 128-channel convolutional, downsamples to one frame per 80 ms |
| Encoder | 8 Simple Attention blocks, Monarch Hadamard MLP |
| Decoder | 8 laddered Simple Attention blocks, width 512, 8 query heads to 2 KV heads |
| Vocabulary | 8,192 text pieces plus 7 language tokens |
| Output ceiling | 320 tokens per clip |
| Input ceiling | 480,000 samples — 30 s at 16 kHz mono |
| Container | `.cact`, Cactus Quants, 2–4 bit |
| Licence | Apache-2.0 |

Three of those rows do more work than the rest, and two of them are the ones
people skip.

### The 9x size headline does not hold precision constant

The comparison everyone quotes is 16.9 MB against Whisper base's 145.3 MB.
That is one ninth the size — but the vendor's own footnote says Whistle is
measured at **2 to 4 bit** while Whisper is **fp32 in memory on the CPU**.
Those are 8–16x apart in bits per weight before a single architectural
decision is made.

The honest apples-to-apples row in the same chart is Moonshine tiny v2 at
**41.9 MB in int8**. Whistle is 2.5x smaller than that, not 9x. The remaining
2.5x is real and is where the architecture earns its keep — but if you repeat
the 9x number you are mostly repeating a quantization choice.

This is the same trap covered in
[how to read an AI benchmark without getting fooled](/docs/models/reading-ai-benchmarks):
the number is not wrong, the *comparison* is not held constant.

### Band-limiting to 3500 Hz is a design decision with a predictable error class

The front end keeps 250–3500 Hz and throws away everything else. That is
roughly telephone band, and it is a sensible choice for a model meant to run
on a wearable — most speech energy lives below it.

Most, not all. The voiceless fricatives and sibilants — the sounds written
`s`, `f`, `th`, `sh` — carry much of their discriminative energy *above* 4 kHz.
Cutting at 3500 Hz does not make them inaudible, but it removes the band that
separates them from each other most cleanly.

So before you run a single test you can predict the error class: confusions
and dropped word-endings on fricatives, worst on speakers whose rhythm or
accent gives those consonants less duration to compensate with. The field
reports in the launch thread — Spanish output with "non-existent words and
terrible typos", Mexican and Venezuelan accents not detected, heavy accents
degrading sharply — are that prediction arriving on schedule.

## The benchmark bars nobody labelled

Here is the part worth the page. Whistle's chart plots word error rate across
nine test sets for three models. It prints a number on **every Whistle bar and
on none of the others**.

The bars are still there, drawn to a linear scale. Whistle's printed labels
calibrate that scale exactly — 6.867 pixels per WER point, consistent to ±0.01
across all nine — so the competitor values are recoverable from bar height.
Doing that produces the table the chart stops just short of giving you.

| Test set | What it is | Whistle | Whisper base ≈ | Moonshine tiny v2 ≈ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LibriSpeech test-clean | Read audiobooks, clean | **4.31** | 4.89 | 4.51 |
| LibriSpeech test-other | Read audiobooks, harder | **10.49** | 11.00 | 11.71 |
| SPGISpeech | Financial calls | **7.65** | — | 7.70 |
| Earnings-22 | Earnings calls, accented | **19.01** | — | 21.25 |
| AMI | Meetings, far-field, overlapping | 26.07 | **21.50** | 22.78 |
| AMI cleaned | Same, cleaned refs | 22.87 | — | — |
| TED-LIUM | Conference talks | 7.61 | **4.99** | 5.64 |
| FLEURS | Multilingual read speech | **21.39** | 24.50 | — |
| MLS | Multilingual audiobooks | 24.90 | **23.10** | — |

Lower is better; bold marks the winner of each row. Whistle's column is the
vendor's printed figures. The other two columns are read off the chart's bars
— the vendor states they are Whisper's and Moonshine's own published numbers,
and declines to print them.

**Whistle wins five of the eight rows where a comparison exists.** For a model
2.5x smaller than its nearest-precision rival that is a genuine result, and
the FLEURS row — 3.1 points better than Whisper base on multilingual read
speech — is the most impressive thing in the chart.

Now look at what it loses:

- **AMI, by 4.57 points to Whisper base.** Far-field microphones, multiple
  speakers, overlap, interruption. Whistle is not merely behind here, it is
  the *worst of the three*, losing to a model 8.6x its size.
- **TED-LIUM, by 2.62 points.** Single speaker, but a large room, a stage mic
  and unscripted delivery.
- **MLS, by 1.80 points.** Multilingual long-form, averaged over six
  non-English languages.

The pattern is not subtle once the numbers are side by side. **Every row
Whistle wins is close-microphone and effectively single-speaker. Every row it
loses has either distance between the mouth and the microphone or more than
one person in the room.** Read-audiobook and dictation audio is exactly the
profile a 16 kHz band-limited front end with a tiny encoder handles well.
Reverberation and overlapping talkers are exactly what it cannot afford the
capacity to disentangle.

That single sentence reconciles the two halves of the launch thread. Everyone
who tested dictation into a phone held near their face liked it. Everyone who
pointed it at a film clip, a meeting or a room disliked it. Nobody was
measuring wrong.

:::tip The number to carry away
4.31 and 26.07 are the same model on the same weights. If you only remember
one WER figure for Whistle, remember the *spread*, not the headline.
:::

### A note on the 70/170 report

The most-quoted field result in the thread — 70 correct out of 170, against
168/170 for a much larger Qwen ASR model — is a real measurement, but it is a
measurement of *stock settings on a closed vocabulary*. The same tester
reported 164/170 after tuning. What changed is the subject of the next
section, and it is the most under-sold feature on the model card.

## Keyword biasing is the primary accuracy lever

Whistle decodes with **five beams scored by length-normalised log
probability**, and it will accept a list of words you expect to hear and
favour them during that search, matched with an Aho-Corasick automaton.

In a general-purpose ASR model this is a convenience. In a 16.9 MB model with
an 8,192-piece vocabulary it is closer to load-bearing. The model has very
little capacity to spend on an implicit language model, so proper nouns,
product names, place names and domain jargon are precisely what it gets wrong
— and precisely what a keyword list fixes. Going from 70/170 to 164/170 is
41% to 96% on the same weights.

<PromptCard title="Transcribe with the two arguments that matter">
{`import needle

r = needle.transcribe(
    "clip.wav",
    keywords=["Kubernetes", "Grafana", "Prometheus", "Aoife", "Nguyen"],
    word_timestamps=True,
    language="en",          # skip detection when you already know
)

print(r["text"])
for w in r["words"]:
    print(w["word"], w["start"], w["end"], w["probability"])`}
</PromptCard>

Three things in that snippet are worth stating plainly:

1. **`keywords` is not a post-processing filter.** It changes the beam search,
   so it can recover a word the decoder would otherwise never have placed.
2. **`language="en"` removes a failure mode, not just latency.** Automatic
   detection across seven languages on a short or noisy clip is one more thing
   that can go wrong. If your app knows the language, say so.
3. **Every word carries its own `probability`.** That is your confidence gate
   — see the handoff pattern below.

<Callout type="warning" items={["A keyword list that is too long dilutes the bias and starts pulling the decoder toward words nobody said. Keep it to the vocabulary your users actually produce in this context — the current screen, the current contact list, the current document — not your entire product glossary."]} />

## The 30-second wall is in the encoder, not in a config file

30 seconds at 16 kHz is 480,000 samples. After the convolutional stem that is
one frame per 80 ms, so 375 encoder frames, and the decoder tops out at 320
output tokens. Those are fixed shapes in a 16.9 MB tensor file. There is no
flag that raises them, and a launch-thread tester who recorded past the limit
got a runtime error rather than a truncated transcript.

So any clip longer than 30 seconds means *you* own the segmentation — and
segmentation is where long-form ASR accuracy actually lives, because a cut
through the middle of a word or a clause is an error the model cannot recover
from.

<Steps items={[

  { title: "Segment on silence, not on a timer", body: "Run a voice-activity detector and cut in the gaps. Fixed 30-second slices will eventually cut mid-word, and that error lands in your transcript permanently. Target 10–25 second segments so you keep headroom for a long final clause." },
  { title: "Overlap the segments by about a second", body: "Give consecutive passes a shared region so you can stitch on agreement instead of trusting a hard boundary. The cost is re-decoding a second of audio; at 1,319 tokens/s decode that is not your bottleneck." },
  { title: "Stitch with the word timestamps, not the text", body: "Every word comes back with a start and an end, so dedupe the overlap region by time rather than by string matching. This is the only reliable way to drop a doubled word without dropping a legitimately repeated one." },
  { title: "Gate on probability and hand off", body: "Average the per-word probabilities over each segment. Low-confidence segments are your queue for a bigger model — on-device for free, off-device when it matters. Do not re-run everything." }

### Silence is handled; low-SNR speech is not

The model card makes a specific and welcome claim: silence and steady noise
return an **empty transcript rather than an invented sentence**, because
silence detection runs *before* the beam search. That is a direct fix for
Whisper's most notorious failure — confidently hallucinating subtitles over
nothing.

Read the claim precisely. It covers silence and steady noise. It does not
cover quiet, distant or overlapping *speech*, and the thread contains exactly
that failure: a tester reported the model emitting "Thank you." repeatedly for
over a minute while real dialogue was playing, and another found a negation
flipped — "can handle" for "can't handle" — plus a word invented outright.

Those are the classic artefacts of training on web video captions, where
"Thanks for watching" occupies an enormous share of the text, and they survive
the silence gate because the gate was never aimed at them. If a dropped `n't`
would change what your application does, a per-word probability check is not
optional.

## Streaming exists — the launch thread concluded it did not

A recurring complaint in the discussion was that Whistle cannot do live
transcription, because the demo showed no partial text while speaking. The
demo did not; the library does. `needle.stream(chunks)` takes live microphone
input and **commits words that consecutive overlapping passes agree on**.

That policy — decode an overlapping window repeatedly, emit only the prefix
two passes agree on — is the LocalAgreement family of streaming ASR, and
knowing that tells you the thing the spec sheet will not:

**Your perceived latency is not the 11.1 ms time-to-first-token.** It is the
agreement delay: how long a word has to wait before a second pass confirms it.
TTFT is a decode-speed measurement on a complete 10-second clip. In a
streaming loop the user-visible number is set by your window stride and how
long you are willing to wait for stability, and it will be hundreds of
milliseconds, not eleven.

Fast is still fast — 11.1 ms against Whisper base's 73.2 ms and Moonshine's
22.8 ms, and 1,319 tokens/s decode against 266 and 262, measured on 10 s of
audio on an Apple M4 Pro CPU. Just do not promise a product manager 11 ms of
live captioning.

## Two features that are quietly more interesting than the size

### The decoder is laddered, so one file is a family

Every depth from two layers up is a deployable model, selected **at load time**
with `--audio-depth`. You download one 16.9 MB blob and choose your
accuracy/latency point on the device, which is the right place to choose it —
a watch and a phone can run the same artifact at different depths. For scale,
the sibling Needle text model decodes at roughly 4,000 tokens/s at two layers
and 400 at twenty on a Raspberry Pi 5.

### `embed()` gives you speech matching with no transcript at all

The encoder output is available directly: one row per 80 ms frame, via
`needle.Whistle().embed(audio)`. That is wake-word spotting, command matching,
audio deduplication and "find where in this recording someone said this" —
without decoding text, without a language model, and without the error classes
discussed above, because you never ask it to spell anything.

For a lot of embedded jobs ("did the user say one of these eleven things?")
this is the correct tool and transcription is the detour.

The `compare` subcommand deserves a mention: the vendor ships the tool that
checks its own claims against your audio. Given how sharply the WER spread
above depends on recording conditions, running it on a representative clip of
*your* audio is worth more than any table on this page.

### One reproducibility property worth copying

The engine **reads no environment variables**. Every behaviour is a compiled
default or an explicit flag. That means the same blob plus the same flags
scores the same on any machine, which is a property most inference stacks
cannot claim and which matters enormously when you are trying to work out
whether last week's eval regressed or last week's shell did.

## Choosing: Whistle, Whisper, Parakeet, or a cloud API

| Your situation | Pick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Dictation on a phone or wearable, close mic, offline, 7 supported languages | **Whistle** | This is the design centre. 16.9 MB, no GPU, word timestamps, and it beats Whisper base on exactly this audio profile |
| Wake words or fixed command sets on a microcontroller | **Whistle `embed()`** | Skip transcription entirely; match encoder frames |
| Meeting recordings, multiple speakers, room mics | **Not Whistle** | Its own chart puts it last on AMI. Use a full-size Whisper, Parakeet, or a cloud ASR with diarization |
| Any language outside the seven | **Not Whistle** | English, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Dutch, Polish only. No Chinese, despite being the most-requested addition in the launch thread |
| Best open-weight accuracy-per-second on a GPU, English | **Parakeet** | NVIDIA's `parakeet-tdt-0.6b-v2` was the practitioner favourite in the thread; CC-BY-4.0 |
| Audio you cannot send anywhere, and accuracy matters more than size | **Whisper large, local** | Still the accuracy reference. You are trading ~100x the footprint for it |
| Medical, legal or financial transcripts of record | **Cloud ASR with a human in the loop** | The negation-flip failure mode above is disqualifying on its own |

A hybrid is usually the right answer, and it is the same shape as every other
local/remote split on this site: run the small model on everything, use the
per-word probabilities as the gate, escalate the uncertain fraction. See
[Claude plus local models](/docs/models/claude-plus-local-models) for the
general pattern and [a private local AI stack](/docs/models/private-local-ai-stack)
for where a transcription step belongs in it.

## What this tells you about the on-device wave generally

Whistle is a good instance of a pattern now showing up across small models:
**the capability is real and the operating envelope is narrow, and only the
first half fits in a headline.** The 2-bit quantization is not a cheat — the
model genuinely works — but it buys its size by spending the capacity that
would have handled hard audio, and the vendor's chart shows exactly where that
bill lands while labelling only the rows where it does not.

The practical rule generalises past ASR: when a small model publishes a wide
benchmark spread, the spread *is* the specification. Find the row where it
does worst, work out what that test set has that the others do not, and check
whether your real input looks like that row. For Whistle the answer is
distance and overlap. For a quantized language model it is usually long
context — see
[what "98.2% retention" hides](/docs/models/ternary-quantization-bonsai-2-27b).
